Processing choices and reporting odds ratios in the FDA quarterly adverse event files: a disproportionality analysis of five SGLT2 and DPP-4 inhibitor associations, 2024 to 2026

Animesh Bose, Norlowe Research

This report has not been peer reviewed.

Abstract

Introduction Disproportionality analyses of the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) quarterly files depend on deduplication, drug-role, and drug-name choices that are rarely tested.

Objective To quantify how reporting odds ratios (RORs) for five previously communicated drug-class and reaction pairs change across prespecified processing configurations, and which counts move.

Methods This disproportionality analysis used eight public quarterly files (2024Q3 to 2026Q2), retaining each case’s latest version after FDA deletions. A factorial design varied the counting unit (case or report version), drug role (primary or any suspect), and ingredient matching (exact or RxNorm-verified expanded) for sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors with ketoacidosis and Fournier’s gangrene, and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors with pemphigoid, arthralgia, and pancreatitis.

Results The files yielded 2,858,073 cases. Across case-level configurations, RORs were 330.3 to 386.9 for Fournier’s gangrene, 51.1 to 90.8 for ketoacidosis, 7.5 to 12.3 for pancreatitis, and 72.3 to 188.5 for pemphigoid. For arthralgia, the ROR was 1.81 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.55 to 2.12) with any suspect role and 0.66 (95% CI 0.47 to 0.94) with primary suspect only; 80% of exposed arthralgia cases qualified only as secondary suspect, mostly in long multi-drug reports (median 41 drugs). Exact matching missed 3,821 SGLT2 and 1,798 DPP-4 inhibitor cases coded under salt or brand names.

Conclusions Some between-configuration differences exceeded a within-configuration CI width; one estimate crossed 1. Analyses should report and test these rules. The estimates describe reporting, not risk.

1 Introduction

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) publishes the contents of its adverse event reporting database for drugs and therapeutic biologics as quarterly data extract files. The files are now listed on FDA’s Adverse Event Monitoring System (AEMS) pages and are still described as FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) quarterly data [1]. They are a common source for case/non-case disproportionality analyses, in which the proportion of reports of an event among reports of a drug is compared with the same proportion among all other reports, often summarized as a reporting odds ratio (ROR) [2, 3]. FDA states that a report does not establish that a drug caused an event and that the reports cannot be used to estimate incidence [4], and its overview of the system for outside analysts notes that duplicate reports inflate counts and can create or mask disproportionality signals [5].

Moving from the raw files to a two-by-two table takes several decisions that published studies often summarize in a sentence. The files deliver one row per report version, keyed by a case identifier (CASEID) and a version number, so an analyst must choose whether to count cases or versions and which version represents a case. Each listed drug carries a reporter-assigned role: primary suspect, secondary suspect, concomitant, interacting, and, from the 2024Q4 extract onward, drug not administered [6]. The drug fields hold a validated trade name or verbatim text and an FDA-assigned active-ingredient field that often uses salt forms, so an analyst must also decide how to match strings to ingredients.

Each of these steps has been studied on its own. Duplicate reports and their characteristics have been described in FAERS [7–9], and probabilistic methods for detecting duplicates have been developed for spontaneous reporting systems [10–12]. Several groups have standardized FAERS drug names, by manual recoding [13], through curated databases [14, 15], or with open dictionaries and RxNorm mappings [16, 17]. The READUS-PV reporting guideline asks authors of disproportionality studies to report how they deduplicated reports, identified drugs, and defined events [18]. Meta-research has found that most disproportionality studies did not report how cases, reactions, or comparators were selected [19], and in VigiBase, the World Health Organization’s global database of individual case safety reports, changing analytic choices such as role restriction or report source produced both positive and negative signals for most pairs in a reference set [20]. FDA’s overview of FAERS discusses duplicates and interpretation but does not specify how users of the public quarterly files should choose drug roles or match drug names [5].

Two recent FAERS studies varied the drug-role rule as a sensitivity analysis [21, 22], and drug-name dictionaries have been compared by how many reports they retrieve [16]. I found no study that measured how much the estimate for a fixed drug-event pair moves when deduplication, role, and name-matching choices change together in the quarterly files, or which units in the table move. I therefore fixed five pairs that FDA has already communicated, so that the target was robustness rather than discovery, and measured how their reporting odds ratios changed across a prespecified set of processing configurations in the eight most recent quarterly files.

2 Methods

2.1 Design and plan

This was a prespecified processing-sensitivity study of publicly available spontaneous reports. The analysis plan, the event term lists, and the configuration set were written and checksummed before any association count was computed. Three dated amendments are reported with the plan (Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM] 1). Two were made before results: one clarified that FDA’s primary-suspect designation rule applies inside both role definitions, and one handled a new role code found during the compatibility review. The third, made after the first results, added descriptive profiles of the units that moved between configurations. No estimate, configuration, or term list was changed after results were seen. The plan was internal and was not registered. ESM 2 maps the applicable READUS-PV items to the manuscript and identifies items not addressed.

2.2 Data source and study window

I downloaded the eight most recent quarterly ASCII (plain-text) extracts listed on FDA’s extract index on 7 October 2026: 2024Q3 to 2026Q2. The newest file (April to June 2026) had been posted on 29 July 2026, and the July to September 2026 file had not yet been posted. The files are noncumulative; each holds the reports FDA received in one quarter. I used the demographic (DEMO), drug (DRUG), reaction (REAC) and outcome (OUTC) files and the deleted-case lists. Each archive was checked for an HTTP 200 response, a non-HTML content type, ZIP structure, and member checksums, and its SHA-256 hash was recorded. All eight releases had identical file headers and layout. The documentation shipped from 2024Q4 onward added one role code, DN (drug not administered) (ESM 2, Table S1). Files were streamed into column-limited Parquet tables without extraction, and parsed row counts for all 40 member files were compared with raw line counts. For the 32 files with a total in FDA’s statistics report, parsed counts were also compared with that total. The eight deletion lists had no printed total.

2.3 Case construction

Each PRIMARYID is one report version; FDA documents it as the concatenation of CASEID and the case version number [6]. When the same PRIMARYID appeared in two extracts, I kept the record from the later extract with that extract’s drug and reaction rows, following FDA’s guidance for such duplicates [23]. FDA describes the latest version of a case as the most current information about it [4]. A case was represented by its latest available version within the acquired files: highest version number, then latest FDA receipt date, then later extract, then larger PRIMARYID. Drugs and reactions came only from the selected version. Cases on any acquired deletion list were removed [23]. “Latest version” therefore means latest within these eight files; later updates were not acquired and earlier versions received before 2024Q3 were not available. Voluntary reports are not linked to manufacturer reports by FDA [23], so duplicates across different CASEIDs were not resolved, and no probabilistic duplicate detection was attempted.

2.4 Drug classes and exposure

Class membership was taken from the RxClass service of the US National Library of Medicine [24]: ingredients assigned the FDA Established Pharmacologic Class (from DailyMed labeling) or the Medication Reference Terminology (MED-RT) mechanism of action for sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibition and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibition. Neither source alone listed every member: ertugliflozin appeared only in MED-RT and bexagliflozin only in the labeling class. The union gave six SGLT2 inhibitors (bexagliflozin, canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, ertugliflozin, sotagliflozin) and four DPP-4 inhibitors (alogliptin, linagliptin, saxagliptin, sitagliptin). The RxNav responses (version 05-Oct-2026) were saved with checksums and reused for every run.

Two matching rules were defined. Under strict matching, a drug row matched when a component of the active-ingredient field (PROD_AI, with multi-ingredient products split on the backslash delimiter) equaled the RxNorm ingredient name. Under expanded matching, a row also matched when a PROD_AI component equaled an RxNorm precise-ingredient name of a member (salt or hydrate forms such as dapagliflozin propanediol or sitagliptin phosphate), or when the drug-name field contained, as whole words, a member’s ingredient name, precise-ingredient name, or a brand name that RxNav relates to it. Strings with no verified match were left unmapped and listed. Same-class ingredients not marketed in the United States (for example vildagliptin) were not members.

Two role rules were defined. Under primary suspect, a row qualified when its role was primary suspect (PS). FDA advises treating the first listed drug as primary suspect when a report has none [23]; I applied this to the lowest drug sequence number that was not coded DN. Under any suspect, PS (including a designated PS) or secondary suspect (SS) qualified. Concomitant, interacting, not-administered, and missing roles never counted. A unit was exposed to a class if any of its drug rows met both the role and the matching rule for any member. Exposure therefore means “listed in the report with this role”, not use, dose, or adherence.

2.5 Events

Each pair used a fixed list of Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) preferred-term (PT) strings, matched exactly after trimming and case folding, with no hierarchy or standardized query (ESM 2, Table S3): ketoacidosis (Diabetic ketoacidosis; Euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis; Ketoacidosis) [25–27]; Fournier’s gangrene [25, 26]; pemphigoid (Pemphigoid) [28, 29]; arthralgia [29]; and pancreatitis (Pancreatitis; Pancreatitis acute; Pancreatitis haemorrhagic; Pancreatitis necrotising) [29]. The pairs were chosen from US prescribing-information warnings. Arthralgia was defined more broadly than the label’s severe, disabling joint pain; severity was not assessed. A unit had the event if any of its reaction rows matched. All ten strings occurred in the data.

2.6 Two-by-two tables and measure

For each pair and configuration, every retained unit fell into exactly one cell: exposed with the event (a), exposed without (b), not exposed with the event (c), and not exposed without (d). The background (c and d) was every other retained unit in the database, all products, built before any restriction to the drug class. Units that listed a class member only in a non-qualifying role stayed in the background; their number is reported. Indicators were built at the unit level, so a report with several drugs and reactions was counted once per pair. The ROR was (a/b)/(c/d) with a Woolf 95% confidence interval (CI) [3]. No estimate was computed if a cell was zero (no continuity correction) or if a was below 3.

2.7 Configurations

The principal set was a full factorial of three two-level factors, giving eight configurations (Table 2). The primary configuration (V1) counted cases, any suspect role, and expanded matching. V2 to V4 changed the matching rule, the role rule, or both. V5 to V8 repeated V1 to V4 with every report version as a unit; they are diagnostic comparators that show what counting follow-up versions does, not equally valid patient-level analyses. Four secondary analyses each changed one element of V1: S1 did not apply deletion lists; S2 kept only cases whose initial FDA receipt date fell within 1 July 2024 to 30 June 2026; S3 compared each class with the other (target-class cases against other-class cases, excluding cases that listed both classes in any role); and S4 removed from the background any unit with an unverified drug string carrying the -gliflozin or -gliptin stem.

For each pair I report the range of RORs over the four case-level configurations, the ratio of the maximum to the minimum, whether the point estimates and lower confidence limits stayed on the same side of 1, and a decomposition of the units that moved between adjacent configurations. The width of a confidence interval within a configuration (precision) and the spread across configurations (robustness) are reported separately. After the first results I added descriptive profiles of the moved units (number of drug and reaction rows per report, literature reference, report type, reporter, and ingredient mix); these are labeled post hoc.

2.8 Validation and reproducibility

Software tests used small synthetic fixtures that exercise version ordering, ties, duplicate PRIMARYIDs, deletions, the primary-suspect designation, DN rows, many-to-many drug and reaction rows, salt and brand matching, and the ROR arithmetic. Source reconciliation compared parsed rows with raw line counts and FDA-printed totals. Structural checks confirmed one selected version per case, that the selected version was the highest, and that the four cells summed to the number of units in every table. A separate pandas and pyarrow implementation, sharing no SQL with the main pipeline, recomputed the case-level tables. The analysis stage was then rerun from cached inputs and compared with a relative tolerance of 10-12. These checks establish software and source consistency; they are not external validation of any association.

2.9 Author verification

Independently of the automated checks, I verified the work by hand. I confirmed the eight archive URLs, posting dates, and SHA-256 hashes against FDA’s extract index and the study manifest, and read FDA’s README, ASC_NTS, and FAQ documents to confirm the version, deletion, and role-code rules that were implemented. For a seeded random sample of 25 cases with more than one version in the window (53 report versions), I compared the raw DEMO rows with the version selected by the pipeline. I reviewed all 348 drug strings that qualified only through the expanded rule against RxNav, checked each reaction string against its cited FDA source, recomputed the primary-configuration ROR and CI for each pair from its four cells, and compared every number in the tables, figures, and text with the run outputs.

2.10 Software

The pipeline was written in Python 3.13.5 with DuckDB 1.5.6 for SQL over Parquet. It runs as six resumable stages (acquire, build, analyze, validate, render, package) on a laptop: on the full input, the build stage took 70 s with a peak resident memory of 2.7 GB, and the analysis stage took 4 s. Figures were drawn with matplotlib and documents rendered with pandoc.

3 Results

3.1 Case construction

The eight files contained 3,253,489 report rows, 15,069,684 drug rows, and 11,287,724 reaction rows. Every parsed count matched the raw line count and, where FDA printed a total (32 files), that total; no line was rejected. A total of 131 PRIMARYIDs appeared in two extracts, leaving 3,253,358 report versions from 2,870,985 cases (Fig. 1, Table 1). Removing 12,912 cases on deletion lists left 2,858,073 cases, and 3,238,049 report versions for the diagnostic unit. In all, 314,495 cases had more than one version in the window. For 667,511 retained cases the selected version was a follow-up whose earlier versions predated the window. No version ties or ordering conflicts occurred among real cases.

Under V1, 26,199 cases were exposed to an SGLT2 inhibitor and 3,919 to a DPP-4 inhibitor (ESM 2, Table S4). Exact matching identified 22,378 and 2,121 of these; the remaining 3,821 and 1,798 were found only through salt-form or brand names. Restricting to primary-suspect listings left 21,573 and 2,076 exposed cases.

3.2 Primary configuration

All five pairs had RORs above 1 under V1 (Table 2): ketoacidosis 82.16 (95% CI 77.43 to 87.18), a = 1,950; Fournier’s gangrene 386.95 (95% CI 319.71 to 468.33), a = 478; pemphigoid 72.27 (95% CI 60.44 to 86.42), a = 136; arthralgia 1.81 (95% CI 1.55 to 2.12), a = 161; and pancreatitis 8.21 (95% CI 6.77 to 9.96), a = 107. The cases in cell a overlapped little between pairs: the largest overlap was 16 cases counted in cell a of both the ketoacidosis and pancreatitis pairs.

3.3 Sensitivity across configurations

Fig. 2 and Table 2 show every configuration. Across the four case-level configurations the ratio of the largest to the smallest ROR was 1.17 for Fournier’s gangrene, 1.64 for pancreatitis, 1.77 for ketoacidosis, 2.61 for pemphigoid, and 4.14 for arthralgia (Table 3). For four pairs every case-level point estimate and lower confidence limit stayed above 1. For arthralgia the estimate fell below 1 when only primary-suspect listings counted: 0.66 (95% CI 0.47 to 0.94) under V3 (a = 32) and 0.72 (95% CI 0.43 to 1.21) under V4 (a = 15).

The spread across configurations was often larger than the width of a single confidence interval. For pemphigoid the 95% interval under V1 spanned 60.4 to 86.4, whereas the case-level point estimates ranged from 72.3 to 188.5. For ketoacidosis the intervals under V1 and V3 did not overlap (77.4 to 87.2 against 47.9 to 54.6).

Counting report versions (V5 to V8) increased the number of units by 379,976 (13.3%) and cell a by 192 for ketoacidosis and 61 for arthralgia. The version-level RORs were lower than the matching case-level RORs for ketoacidosis (69.5 against 82.2), Fournier’s gangrene, pemphigoid, and pancreatitis, and slightly higher for arthralgia with any suspect role (1.93 against 1.81); no version-level estimate changed side of 1 relative to its case-level counterpart.

3.4 What moved between configurations

Drug role. Moving from any suspect to primary suspect (V1 to V3) removed from the exposed group 4,626 SGLT2 inhibitor cases and 1,843 DPP-4 inhibitor cases in which the drug was only a secondary suspect (Table 3). These included 684 of the 1,950 ketoacidosis cases and 129 of the 161 arthralgia cases (Fig. 3). The arthralgia cases that qualified only as secondary suspect differed from those that qualified as primary suspect (post hoc; ESM 2, Table S7): the median report listed 41 drugs (interquartile range 29 to 88) and 66 reactions, 83% listed 20 or more drugs, and 98% were expedited reports, against a median of 4 drugs and 6.5 reactions among primary-suspect cases. Secondary-suspect-only cases also listed more drugs than primary-suspect cases for the other pairs, but less extremely (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Drug-name matching. Moving from expanded to strict matching (V1 to V2) removed 3,821 SGLT2 and 1,798 DPP-4 inhibitor cases from the exposed group. Almost all were coded under a salt name in PROD_AI; cases identified only from the drug-name field were few (2 DPP-4 inhibitor cases and 0 SGLT2 inhibitor cases under the suspect rule). The cases found only by expanded matching reported the target events less often than the strictly matched cases (post hoc; ESM 2, Table S7): ketoacidosis 2.1% against 8.4%, and pemphigoid 1.3% against 5.3%. The two groups differed in ingredient mix: for DPP-4 inhibitors, strictly matched cases were split between sitagliptin and linagliptin, whereas expanded-only cases were mostly sitagliptin coded as sitagliptin phosphate. Including them therefore lowered the pemphigoid ROR from 110.3 (V2) to 72.3 (V1) and the ketoacidosis ROR from 90.8 to 82.2.

Non-qualifying listings. Under V1, 25,352 cases listed an SGLT2 inhibitor and 9,379 listed a DPP-4 inhibitor only as concomitant, interacting, not administered, or without a role, and stayed in the background. They contributed 174 of the 2,769 background ketoacidosis cases and 307 of the 65,942 background arthralgia cases.

3.5 Secondary analyses

Not applying deletion lists (S1) changed no ROR by more than 1.8% (Table 4). Restricting to cases first received in the window (S2, 2,638,448 cases) changed the other estimates by at most 7% but lowered the arthralgia ROR to 1.43 (95% CI 1.17 to 1.75). Removing unverified same-stem strings from the background (S4) changed the pemphigoid ROR from 72.27 to 75.09 and the others by less. In the class-against-class comparison (S3), which excluded 1,638 cases listing both classes, the RORs were 5.12 (95% CI 3.75 to 7.00) for ketoacidosis, 26.84 (95% CI 6.69 to 107.73) for Fournier’s gangrene (c = 2), 45.77 (95% CI 29.24 to 71.64) for pemphigoid, 2.12 (95% CI 1.55 to 2.91) for arthralgia, and 2.00 (95% CI 1.51 to 2.65) for pancreatitis.

3.6 Validation

All 40 member files reconciled, every structural check passed, and the pandas implementation reproduced all 20 case-level tables exactly. The rerun from cached inputs reproduced every count and estimate exactly. All 18 software tests passed. A naive join of drug and reaction rows would have produced 2,114 rows for the 1,950 ketoacidosis cases in cell a under V1.

4 Discussion

4.1 Main findings

Across eight prespecified processing configurations, two of five previously communicated pairs were stable within a factor of about 1.2 to 1.6 (Fournier’s gangrene and pancreatitis), two moved by a factor of about 1.8 to 2.6 while staying well above 1 (ketoacidosis and pemphigoid), and one changed direction (arthralgia). The changes came from identifiable groups of reports: secondary-suspect listings, often in reports naming many drugs, and cases whose ingredient was coded under a salt name. Counting report versions instead of cases inflated the counts by about 13% but changed the conclusions less than the role and name rules did. In this window the deletion lists mattered little.

4.2 Comparison with previous work

Most attention in FAERS data preparation has gone to duplicates. FDA notes that duplicate reports inflate counts and can create or mask signals [5]; in an FDA sample, 39% of reports were duplicates, more often foreign or literature-derived [7]; literature reports can be repeated across many cases [8, 9]; and FDA’s network-based deduplication of the full database flagged about 5 million of 29 million reports as duplicates [30].

These results agree that counting report versions inflates counts but suggest that, for these pairs, it is not the largest source of variation. CASEID collapsing cannot detect duplicates that FDA has not linked [12], and the secondary-suspect arthralgia reports, with a median of 41 drugs each, resemble the long multi-drug reports described in the extreme-duplication work; I did not test whether they duplicate one another.

The drug-role result has close precedents. In VigiBase, varying analytic choices including role restriction produced both positive and negative signals for most pairs in a reference set [20]. In FAERS, primary-suspect stratification identified 9 of the 30 highest-ranked glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist signals as driven by secondary-suspect reports [22], and an analysis of the 2025 releases used a primary-suspect cohort with a secondary-suspect sensitivity cohort [21]. The arthralgia result here is a further example, for an event named in US labeling: its estimate was above 1 only when secondary-suspect listings counted.

Drug-name standardization for FAERS has been approached by manual recoding [13], curated databases [14, 15], and open dictionaries and RxNorm mappings [16, 17]. The authors of the AEOLUS resource noted that cleaning and normalization choices can change analysis results without quantifying the effect [14], and the DiAna authors suggested comparing disproportionality results across standardization strategies [16]. For the ten ingredients studied, the difference between exact and RxNorm-informed matching was mainly salt-form coding in the ingredient field, and the salt-coded reports had a different reaction profile. Exact matching therefore did not lose cases at random; it changed which reports formed the exposed group.

The four labeled associations other than arthralgia were above 1 in every case-level configuration, as in earlier analyses: ketoacidosis with SGLT2 inhibitors in FAERS [31, 32], Fournier’s gangrene in an FDA case series and in FAERS [33, 34], pemphigoid with DPP-4 inhibitors in French and FAERS data [35, 36], and pancreatitis with sitagliptin in the earlier Adverse Event Reporting System (AERS) [37, 38], an association later reviewed by FDA and the European Medicines Agency [39]. I did not compare magnitudes because periods, case definitions, and backgrounds differ. The French study found a much higher ROR for vildagliptin than for sitagliptin [35]; same-class ingredients not marketed in the United States sit in the background of the primary analysis here, and removing them (S4) raised the pemphigoid ROR slightly.

For arthralgia, a Spanish pharmacovigilance study reported an ROR of 2.69 [40] and a meta-analysis of randomized trials a relative risk of 1.13 [41]. In these data the estimate was above 1 only when secondary-suspect listings in long multi-drug reports counted; the primary-suspect estimate was below 1. A disproportionality estimate for the broad arthralgia term in a recent window does not test the severe, disabling joint pain described in the labeling [29].

The class-against-class comparison (S3) answered a different question, because both classes are used for type 2 diabetes. Its lower RORs are consistent with part of the database-wide contrast reflecting the indication rather than the drug, but they are still reporting associations.

4.3 Implications for analyses of the quarterly files

READUS-PV already asks for the deduplication, drug-identification, and event-definition rules [18]. For studies using the quarterly files, these results support reporting choices that readers can inspect: report the drug-role rule and test the alternative; include precise-ingredient (salt) names when matching the ingredient field, or use a published standardization; and show the four cells, not only the ROR, so readers can see which counts drive an estimate. When a role or name rule changes the conclusion, the units responsible can usually be characterized, as here, and should be described. The pipeline used here runs these comparisons from configuration files and can be pointed at other quarters, drugs, or event lists.

4.4 Limitations

Spontaneous reports lack an exposure denominator and are shaped by stimulated reporting after safety communications, by selective reporting, and by the indication; the RORs here are reporting associations and cannot be read as risks, incidence, or causal effects.

CASEID collapsing does not remove duplicates that FDA has not linked, such as a direct report and a manufacturer report of the same patient. The window contained only the versions received in eight quarters, so case histories are incomplete; 667,511 retained cases were represented by a follow-up whose earlier versions predated the window, and the receipt-date cohort (S2) addresses this only partly.

The event lists were exact preferred-term strings without the MedDRA hierarchy, so related terms outside the lists were not counted. The configurations cover three choices; others, such as country, reporter type, or literature reports, were not varied.

The descriptive profiles that explain the role and mapping effects were added after the first results and are post hoc. Five pairs in two classes cannot show how often these effects occur in general.

FDA codes reactions with the MedDRA version current when each extract is produced [4], so preferred-term strings can change between quarters; all ten strings were present, but a renamed term would not have been counted. Class-level estimates pool ingredients with different reporting histories, and ingredient-level estimates were not computed. I carried out the verification in Sect. 2.9; it was not an independent external review.

5 Conclusions

In the eight most recent FDA quarterly files, the reporting odds ratio for a known drug-event pair depended on which drug roles counted as exposure and how drug names were matched, with variation across configurations sometimes exceeding a within-configuration confidence-interval width. For arthralgia, the estimate crossed the null value of 1. Counting report versions inflated the counts but mattered less. Analyses of these files should state and test their role and name rules, show the cells behind each estimate, and describe the reports that move when a rule changes. These results concern the processing of reporting data. They do not establish whether the drugs caused these events.
