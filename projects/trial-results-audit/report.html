Row-level and unit-level participant counts in ClinicalTrials.gov results and what one-count-per-arm extraction attaches instead: a cross-sectional study of cardiometabolic trials

Animesh Bose, Norlowe Research

This report has not been peer reviewed. Corrected and extended on 25 October 2026: it now follows the revised manuscript, which corrects the descriptions of the checks and of the use of AI tools and adds a post hoc analysis of two-arm comparisons.

Supplementary material: analysis plan, rules, amendment 4 and its run record (Additional file 1), data dictionary (Additional file 2), source archive, version 1.0.0 (Additional file 3 of the manuscript is version 1.1.0, which adds amendment 4 and its analysis), result tables workbook (Additional file 4), issue ledger, claims map.

Abstract

Background. Evidence tables from ClinicalTrials.gov results need the participant count behind each value. The registry may give it per outcome, per table row or beside a count of other units; one-count-per-arm extraction holds only the first. I measured how often written rules can attach the applicable count, what such an extraction attaches instead, and what that does to two-arm comparisons.

Methods. Interventional diabetes, heart failure and obesity trials with results first posted January 2020 to September 2026 were retrieved by condition search. Of these, 2,469 met a condition vocabulary rule and were split by identifier cluster into development (508) and held-out (1,961) portions; rules and code were frozen by hash and run once on the held-out portion. Outcome groups were classed as directly structured, needing an explicit rule, or not safely resolvable. The comparator was a declared flat export carrying each arm's outcome-level count; a post hoc amendment recomputed affected two-arm comparisons with those counts. A second R implementation, written with the same artificial intelligence coding assistants, checked every value.

Results. Of 43,715 applicable held-out outcome groups, 43,710 (99.99%) could be given a participant count: 37,592 (86.0%) directly and 6,118 (14.0%) by an explicit rule, mostly for row-level numbers analyzed. For 22,505 of 97,756 measurements (23.0%), the flat export attached the outcome-level count although the row reported a different number analyzed. Post hoc, these came from 385 of 1,911 studies (20.1%) and made up 11.1% of primary-outcome measurements. In 1,052 affected two-arm comparisons of proportions (post hoc), the export's counts shifted the risk difference by a median of 0.37 percentage points (over 5 points in 9.3%) and changed its 95% interval's relation to zero in 21 (2.0%). The warehouse reproduced all 2,502,817 in-scope source values; the R implementation agreed on every measurement.

Conclusions. Written rules attached a participant count to almost every value, but one outcome group in seven needed a row-level or unit-level rule. A one-count-per-arm export attached a different count to nearly a quarter of measurements; post hoc, most risk differences changed little. Evidence pipelines should keep every denominator level and choose among them by explicit rules.

Keywords: ClinicalTrials.gov; trial results; denominators; analysis population; data extraction; data provenance; evidence synthesis; research methodology

Background

ClinicalTrials.gov has accepted summary results of registered trials since 2008 [1,2], and the Final Rule extended results reporting to more trials from 2017 [3]. The results records are now reused at scale: systematic reviewers extract posted values [4], meta-researchers analyze reporting across thousands of trials [5], and tools republish the data in other forms. Posted results have also been used to emulate rapid safety meta-analyses [6] and to build a benchmark of 12,967 drug-versus-drug adverse event comparisons from 33,701 trial records [7]. A posted value means little without the number of participants it describes. Zarin and colleagues described the outcome measure as the "numerator" of a result and the analysis population as its "denominator", and found that the number of distinct analysis populations per study group averaged 2.5 across 700 early results records [1].

Studies comparing registry results with other sources keep returning to the denominator. Hartung and colleagues found primary outcome results inconsistent with the publication in 20% of sampled trials, including inconsistent analysis denominators and transposed arms [8]. Comparisons with trials published in high-impact journals also found results that could not be compared directly because the two sources reported them in different forms [9,10]. Schwartz and colleagues found the number analyzed for the primary outcome discordant with U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) reviews for 31% of primary outcomes, in a sample of approval trials of which 42% were for diabetes [11]. Riveros and colleagues found the number analyzed per group reported in 96% of sampled posted results but reasons for exclusion in only 8% [12], and Dechartres and colleagues could recompute effects from posted per-arm values for only 65% of trials that posted neither an estimate nor a p-value [13]. Registry staff have explained that results tables resist spreadsheet download because their rows and columns vary from record to record [14], and that internal inconsistencies between parts of a record are among the most frequent problems at results quality review [14,15].

Several tools already restructure posted results. AACT (Aggregate Analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov), built by the Clinical Trials Transformation Initiative [16], now loads the whole registry into PostgreSQL every day [17]. EXACT (EXtracting Accurate efficacy and safety information from ClinicalTrials.gov) extracts results into spreadsheets for meta-analysis [18], Shi and Du built an arm-level knowledge graph of efficacy and safety results [19], and the ctrdata package stores registry records, including results, as documents for analysis in R [20]. CTG-DB (ClinicalTrials.gov Transformation Database), released as a preprint in 2026, converts the registry's archive into a relational database for cross-trial adverse event analyses and keeps one denominator per arm [21]. Others have examined the quality of the registry entries themselves. Miron and colleagues found untyped coded fields, values that differed from the data dictionary and condition terms outside Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) in the protocol metadata, without examining the summary results [22]. Iken and colleagues proposed a structured assessment of consistency, accuracy, completeness and timeliness and, in 816 knee arthroplasty trials, found inconsistent entries for up to 36% of trials, depending on the item, and no reported outcome for 61% of finished trials [23]. The tools show that restructuring is feasible; the quality studies show that registry entries need checking before reuse. None of this work reports how often each posted value can be paired with its own participant count without unsupported inference, or what a simpler representation attaches in its place.

The registry's own definitions make the question concrete. Each posted outcome has an "Overall Number of Participants Analyzed" per group; a table row can carry its own number analyzed "if different from the overall"; outcomes analyzed in eyes, clinics or other units report a unit count beside the participant count; and the analysis population description is conditionally required [24]. A representation that keeps one count per arm and outcome cannot hold these distinctions.

For a frozen cohort of cardiometabolic trials, loaded into a typed warehouse that keeps each value's source location, I asked: among posted results, how often can a documented participant denominator be preserved and mechanically associated with each reported value without unsupported inference, and what does a declared one-row-per-value flat export lose or attach instead? A post hoc amendment then asked how much the attached counts change comparisons between two arms. This is a study of machine-readable reporting and transformation fidelity. It does not judge whether treatments work, detect misconduct or adjudicate conditions clinically.

Methods

Aim, design and setting

This cross-sectional methods evaluation used one snapshot of the ClinicalTrials.gov registry (data timestamp 2026-10-06). Its aim was to measure how often written rules can attach a participant denominator to each posted results value without unsupported inference, and what a flat export attaches instead. Rules were developed on one portion of the cohort and run once on a held-out portion.

Data source and cohort

I used the ClinicalTrials.gov data application programming interface (API), version 2.0.5 [25,26]. The data timestamp was 2026-10-06T09:00:05 Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) both before and after retrieval, which began at 2026-10-07T09:33:06Z. Three condition queries were passed verbatim to the registry's condition search ("diabetes mellitus", "heart failure", "obesity") with the filter AREA[StudyType]INTERVENTIONAL AND AREA[ResultsFirstPostDate]RANGE[2020-01-01,2026-09-30]. The window, chosen before retrieval as 2020 through the last complete calendar month, refers to the date results were first posted after quality review; it is not the study completion date. Every query was paged to the end and its identifier count checked against the API's reported total (1,511, 468 and 842 studies). The union of 2,615 studies was below the prespecified ceiling of 5,000 studies, so no sampling was needed. Full records were retrieved in batches of 100 identifiers, and every raw page was saved with its Secure Hash Algorithm 256-bit (SHA-256) hash. Responses that were not JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) were rejected.

The registry's condition search also matches titles, keywords and MeSH ancestor terms. The pipeline therefore confirmed each study's condition group by a deterministic, prespecified rule: the condition MeSH or ancestor terms contained the group's descriptor (Diabetes Mellitus, Heart Failure or Obesity), or a free-text condition entry matched the group's pattern. Studies whose condition entries and condition MeSH terms did not meet the rule (146 studies; returned through titles, keywords, other MeSH terms or condition text the rule does not accept) were set aside and used only in a sensitivity analysis. This is a vocabulary rule, not clinical adjudication. No study failed the frame checks (interventional, results present, results first posted inside the window), so 2,469 confirmed studies remained (1,440 diabetes, 435 heart failure, 762 obesity; 166 in more than one group).

Development and held-out portions

Before any results field was parsed, studies were linked into clusters when they shared an alias National Clinical Trial (NCT) number (the ClinicalTrials.gov identifier), an organization study identifier within the same organization, a secondary identifier, an identical official title, or near-identical brief titles (token Jaccard similarity of at least 0.9; 32 pairs). A first pass let bare values such as "2017", entered as secondary identifiers, join unrelated studies; identifiers with fewer than five alphanumeric characters were then ignored, and the split was regenerated (2026-10-07T09:37:42Z) before any results were loaded (first load 09:41:39Z; amendment 1). The 2,525 clusters were assigned by a salted SHA-256 hash of their smallest NCT identifier, about 20% to development: 508 confirmed studies for development and 1,961 held-out.

The loader, checks and analysis code were written with artificial intelligence (AI) coding assistants; the code and the written rules were developed and reviewed on the development portion only. Rule wording and an additional check of category sums were recorded after the development portion had been profiled (analysis plan, amendment 2). Two further checks, of adverse event group totals and of non-zero values with zero participants analyzed, were also defined on the development portion before the freeze; a note added to the plan after the held-out run records them. AI agents then reviewed the development code against the written rules, one agent for each part of the code, and the review workflow assigned each reported defect to a further agent to re-check. The findings file saved during the review lists 35 reported defects, of which 16 had been reproduced, 2 refuted and 17 not yet re-checked; no later findings file exists (amendment 4). The corrections were recorded before the freeze (amendment 3). No other person reviewed the code. The files that define the computation were then hashed (combined SHA-256 5bd267b67dc1f28e…, 2026-10-07T10:53:24Z), and the primary held-out analysis was run once, at 2026-10-07T11:03:34Z. The program refuses a second primary run and allows only a logged reproducibility rerun, which writes to a separate directory and is compared with the original tables.

An analysis plan dated 2026-10-07T09:30Z, before retrieval began at 09:33:06Z, is given with its dated amendments in Additional file 1. These times are written in the plan itself, which was not hashed before the held-out run and received its notes and post hoc additions after that run; the cohort settings it fixes (posting window, condition rule and 5,000-study ceiling) are in config/cohort.yaml, saved at 09:30:20Z and included in the freeze hash. It is an internal record, not a public preregistration. Amendment 4, written on 24 October 2026 after all results were known, corrects how the plan describes the code review and the R reference and specifies the post hoc consequence analysis described below (Additional file 1, Part C).

Warehouse

Results were loaded into native PostgreSQL 17 tables for studies and module-scoped groups; participant flow periods, milestones and reasons not completed; baseline denominators, measures, rows, categories and values; outcome measures, groups, outcome-level and row-level denominators, rows, categories and values; statistical analyses with their comparison groups; and adverse event groups, terms and counts.

Group identifiers are keyed within their module, and outcome group identifiers within their outcome measure, so the same code in two outcomes is never assumed to be the same population. Every value is stored as the exact source text, with a parsed number only when the text is a plain decimal written in American Standard Code for Information Interchange (ASCII) digits; every row stores its JSON pointer into the source record.

Composite keys and foreign keys enforce the nesting. A load replaces the studies in scope inside one transaction, so loading a frozen snapshot twice gives identical tables, and a failed load leaves the previous state intact.

The results point-of-contact block (names, e-mail addresses, telephone numbers) and the certain-agreement block (restrictions on investigators publishing results) were not loaded. A staging table keeps the rest of each record as retrieved, including the protocol section; the analysis does not use it. The source-to-warehouse data dictionary is given in Additional file 2.

Attaching a denominator

For each posted outcome measurement I applied written rules, in a fixed order (Additional file 1). The analysis unit is the outcome's selected unit, or participants when none is given. I treated an association as made without unsupported inference when it used only counts the record states for that group, with the precedence the registry's data element definitions give: a row reports its own number analyzed only "if different from the overall" count [24], so when no row reports one, the outcome-level count applies to every row, and a row's own count takes precedence where it is given.

Reading a count from free text, borrowing one from another outcome or module, or converting a non-integer entry such as "NA" was treated as unsupported (tier C). When only some rows of an outcome report their own count, the convention would give the other rows the outcome-level count. The record cannot show whether those rows were left blank on purpose, so this case was also treated as unsupported.

Tier A: one usable participant count for the group at the outcome level, no row-level counts, analysis in participants. Tier B: a deterministic rule is needed, either because the outcome's rows carry their own number analyzed, which then takes precedence (B1), or because the outcome is analyzed in another unit and the participant count is held separately from the unit count (B2). Tier C: no participant count can be attached without unsupported inference. This covers no count for the group (C1), a count that is not an integer, for example "NA" (C2), more than one count (C3), row-level counts on only some rows (C4), row-level counts only in other units (C5) and no usable count in the selected unit (C6). Tier D: the outcome is not posted or the group has no values.

An outcome group takes its least favorable measurement tier. The primary estimand is the distribution of held-out outcome groups across tiers A, B and C; tier D is reported separately.

Whether the outcome has an analysis population description is reported alongside, as present or absent; absence is not an error, because the field is required only when the number analyzed differs from the number assigned [24]. Population labels such as "modified intention to treat" are themselves used inconsistently [27], so the rules never read the denominator from the description text.

Comparator

The comparator is a declared flat export in the shape produced by the pandas function json_normalize along each module's deepest list: one comma-separated values (CSV) row per value, with every scalar of its ancestors (module, outcome or measure, group, row, category) copied down, values kept as text, and the outcome-level participant count attached to each row. A declared export was needed because the registry's own CSV download carries no posted results data, only a flag for whether results exist and the date they were first posted [28].

A source value counts as recoverable from the flat files only when exactly one row matches its full context and a cell holds the identical text. Statistical analysis rows carry no context below their outcome, so they were matched by their order within the outcome, and only when the outcome's 13 descriptive fields (type, title, description, population description, time frame, units and similar) taken together are unique in the study. Amendment 2 (item 4) of the analysis plan named only type, title and time frame. The frozen code requires all 13 fields together to be unique, so 27 outcomes with analyses in 6 held-out studies that share type, title and time frame with another outcome, but differ in other fields, were also matched by order (Additional file 1).

Fidelity reference

A second program, in R (version 4.5.1, jsonlite 2.0.0) and written with the same AI coding assistants from the written tier rules, read the same raw pages, enumerated every scalar under each results section with its JSON pointer, counted every array, and applied the tier rules in its own code. Agreement between the warehouse and this reference measures transformation fidelity. Because both follow the same written rules and came from the same assistants, a shared misreading of those rules would not be detected. Each results module was also rebuilt from the warehouse and compared with the source record as canonical JSON.

Checks for possible source inconsistencies

Twelve checks (K1 to K12) were run only where units and populations permit them. They looked for participant counts above their participant denominator (K1), unit counts above their unit denominator (K2), interval limits out of order (K3), a point value outside its interval (K4) and percentages of participants outside 0 to 100 (K5). For adverse events they looked for participants affected above participants at risk (K6) or above the number of events (K7), and group totals above participants at risk (K8). They also covered baseline counts above their denominator (K9), two-sided confidence intervals of statistical analyses whose limits are reversed or exclude the estimate (K10), non-zero values with zero participants analyzed (K11), and category counts that do not add up to the participants analyzed for their row (K12; categories are defined as mutually exclusive and exhaustive [24]).

Values that were present but not plain numbers were counted as not evaluable, as was a point value whose interval limits were out of order, since the limit-order check already reports that case. Reversed confidence limits of a statistical analysis, which the limit-order check does not cover, counted as a failure of the analysis check. A failure is a possible source inconsistency, not a confirmed error. Sums across rows, comparisons between outcome, baseline and participant-flow counts, and whether a mean, median or survival estimate should carry a binary event denominator were deliberately not checked. Every flag enters an issue ledger with its JSON pointer, the source values and the rule that fired.

Analysis

Counts are reported with their unit: study, study module, outcome, outcome group, measurement, source scalar, object or applicable check. The cohort is the complete set of confirmed studies among those the three queries returned, not a sample of all trials, and the held-out portion is a fixed hash partition of it. Percentages therefore describe this finite set of studies and carry no sampling intervals; a sensitivity analysis gives the shares for both portions combined. An exploratory bootstrap resampling whole trials (1,911 held-out studies with at least one applicable outcome group; 2,000 replicates, seed 20261007) shows how much the outcome-group tier shares depend on trial composition; its percentile range is not a confidence interval.

Four sensitivity analyses were fixed in advance: including ambiguous condition matches (SA1), primary outcomes only (SA2), counting a group as resolvable only if its outcome also has a population description (SA3), and both portions combined (SA4). I also described how often an outcome group's title exactly matches a baseline group title in the same study, and whether matching titles carry the same participant count. Among studies that use the group code OG000 in two or more outcomes, I counted those that give it different titles in different outcomes, comparing titles after case folding and whitespace collapsing. Results labeled post hoc were added after the held-out run, in the plan's post hoc additions or in amendment 4; they describe the held-out results further and change no prespecified result.

Consequence for two-arm comparisons (post hoc)

Amendment 4 (Additional file 1, Part C) was written on 24 October 2026, after all held-out results were known and after AI agents had looked at rough magnitudes on 20 October. It fixed the design, and the threshold for calling the consequence material, before the analysis code was written. A cell is one row and category of an outcome. It is affected when, for at least one group, the row reports a number analyzed that differs from that group's outcome-level count, and it is a two-arm comparison when the outcome has exactly two groups. Each affected two-arm cell posted as a count of participants, that is, a proportion in each arm, was analyzed twice: with the row-level counts, and with each arm's outcome-level count, as a one-count-per-arm export would supply it. Both analyses gave the risk difference with a 95% Newcombe hybrid score interval [29] and, where both arms had at least one participant with the outcome, the risk ratio. For comparisons in which an arm had no participant with the outcome, a sensitivity analysis added 0.5 to each of the four counts of the two-by-two table. For means reported with a standard deviation, the standard error of the difference, its inverse-variance weight and the 95% interval of the mean difference were recomputed under each count. For means reported with a standard error, the interval does not depend on the count, so only the standard deviation that would be derived from the standard error was compared.

The consequence was to be called material if at least 5% of affected comparisons of proportions changed their risk-difference interval's relation to zero, or if the median absolute change in the risk difference reached 1 percentage point; otherwise it was to be called small. Cells in outcomes with one group or with three or more groups, in tier C, in outcomes analyzed in other units, with values that were not plain numbers, or with zero participants in an arm were counted and excluded. Primary outcomes were summarized separately, and the analysis was repeated without the study with the most measurements whose row-level count differed (NCT02443155, the worked example below) and without the ten studies with the most. An exploratory bootstrap that resampled whole trials (2,000 replicates, seed 20261024) gave a percentile range for the share of changed intervals; as with the bootstrap above, the range is not a confidence interval. A new module, reading only the project's exported tables and frozen study list, ran the analysis on 24 October 2026, after amendment 4 had been hashed (Additional file 3). Another R program, written by a separate request to the same AI model from a written specification, read the raw registry pages and recomputed every figure reported below.

Most outcome groups have a directly structured denominator, and one in seven needs a rule

Of 43,715 applicable outcome groups, 37,592 (86.0%) were tier A, 6,118 (14.0%) tier B and 5 (0.01%) tier C; 1,885 groups were not applicable (Figure 1, Table 2). Most of tier B came from row-level numbers analyzed: all 5,578 groups in outcomes that reported them were B1, and in 4,795 of them at least one row's count differed from the outcome-level count (post hoc). In tables with more than one row, 5,641 of 13,399 groups (42.1%) needed a rule, against 1.6% of groups in single-row outcomes. Of 582 groups in outcomes analyzed in other units (for example clinics, eyes or samples), 540 used the unit rule (B2), 37 the row-level rule (B1) and 5 were tier C. These were all the tier C groups: in 3 studies whose units were clinics or community organizations, the participant count was "NA" while the count of clinics or organizations was given.

What a flat export attaches

For 62,363 of 97,756 measurements (63.8%), the flat export attached the count that applies (Figure 2). For 22,505 (23.0%) it attached the outcome-level count while the row reported a different number analyzed, and for 12,074 (12.4%) the row-level count happened to equal it. For 799 (0.8%) it attached a participant count to values reported per clinic, eye or other unit; that count is correct as a participant count, but it is not the denominator of those values. For the 5 measurements with no safe count, the export's count cell held the source text "NA", and 10 measurements could not be located uniquely in the flat file. In total, 23,309 measurements (23.8%) carried an entry that was not the most specific participant count, was not in the unit of the value, or was "NA". The 22,505 measurements whose row reported a different number analyzed came from 385 of the 1,911 held-out studies with posted values (20.1%) and 2,084 of the 18,110 outcomes with values (11.5%). The ten studies contributing most accounted for 8,450 of them (37.5%), and among measurements of primary outcomes the share was 2,260 of 20,271 (11.1%). These four descriptions are post hoc. The flat export also had no row at all for 1,847 of 19,957 outcomes (9.3%): the 1,152 outcomes not yet posted and the 695 posted without values. No outcome with posted values lacked a row.

Two-arm comparisons with the export's counts (post hoc)

The 22,505 measurements whose row reported a different number analyzed fell into 9,706 affected cells. Two-arm comparisons cover only part of them: 3,952 cells (13,307 measurements) were in outcomes with three or more groups, 1,214 in one-group outcomes, and 518 had a value for only one of two groups. Of the remaining two-arm cells, 1,052 were comparisons of proportions, 2,088 means with standard deviations and 196 means with standard errors; 637 used other parameter or dispersion types, and 49 were excluded because the outcome was analyzed in another unit (26), a value or dispersion was not a plain number (15) or an arm had zero participants (8). The full flow is in Additional file 4, sheet Post hoc A4 cell flow. No row-level count was larger than its outcome-level count; the median ratio of the two was 0.84 (interquartile range 0.66–0.92), and it was below half for 3,442 of the 22,505 measurements (15.3%).

In 1,052 affected comparisons of proportions from 62 studies, the export's counts changed the risk difference by a median of 0.37 percentage points (interquartile range 0.03–1.45), by more than 1 point in 358 cells (34.0%) and by more than 5 points in 98 (9.3%; Table 8). The 95% interval changed its relation to zero in 21 cells (2.0%) from 14 studies: 11 excluded zero with the row-level counts but not with the export's, 9 the reverse, and 1 excluded zero on opposite sides. An exploratory whole-trial bootstrap put this share between 1.1% and 3.7%. Risk ratios changed by 10% or more in 141 of the 714 cells with events in both arms (19.7%), because the row-level count was a different fraction of the outcome-level count in the two arms; that fraction differed by more than 5% in 446 of the 1,052 cells (42.4%). When 0.5 was added to each count of the two-by-two table in the other 338 comparisons, which had no participant with the outcome in at least one arm, a further 94 risk ratios changed by 10% or more (235 of 1,052 in all, 22.3%). Both measures named in amendment 4 were below their thresholds (2.0% against 5%, and 0.37 against 1 percentage point), so by the amendment's rule the consequence is called small. Primary outcomes contributed 149 cells, of which 1 changed its interval's relation to zero. Excluding NCT02443155 or the ten largest contributors left the share of changed intervals at 2.0% and 2.1% (Table 8).

For 2,088 mean differences reported with standard deviations (155 studies), the 95% interval changed its relation to zero in 81 (3.9%). Because the export's counts are never smaller than the rows' own, they can only shrink the standard error: the inverse-variance weight of the difference rose by a median factor of 1.12 (interquartile range 1.05–1.31), and by more than 10% in 1,157 of the 2,087 cells with a non-zero standard error (55.4%). Where only standard errors were posted (196 cells), the interval does not depend on the count, but a standard deviation derived as the standard error times √n would be too large by a median factor of 1.04 across the 392 values in these cells (interquartile range 1.01–1.09). The R recomputation agreed on all 120 figures compared; the bootstrap ranges, drawn with different random number generators, agreed within 0.1 percentage point.

Discussion

In 1,961 held-out diabetes, heart failure and obesity trials with results first posted from 2020 to 2026, a participant denominator could be attached by written rules to 99.99% of applicable outcome groups. The difficulty lies in where the count sits: 14.0% of groups, and 36.2% of individual measurements, carried their count at row level or beside a unit count. A flat export with one count per arm and outcome attached the outcome-level count in place of a different row-level number analyzed to 23.0% of measurements, and a participant count without the unit count to another 0.8%. Once an export omits row-level counts, its users cannot recover those counts from the exported values alone.

These results extend the denominator framing of Zarin and colleagues [1] from a sample of early records to every posted value in a recent cohort. Schwartz and colleagues found the number analyzed discordant with FDA reviews for about a third of primary outcomes [11]; part of such discordance may arise when one source reports a row-level count and the other an overall count, although these data cannot show how much. Dechartres and colleagues could not recompute effects from posted per-arm values for 35% of trials that posted neither an estimate nor a p-value, mainly because binary outcomes were posted only as percentages, continuous outcomes lacked a mean and standard deviation, or the data were censored or repeated over time [13]. Missing participant counts were not among the main reasons they named, and in this cohort these counts were almost always present. For 23.0% of all measurements, however, and 11.1% of measurements of primary outcomes (post hoc), the row reported its own number analyzed, which differed from the outcome-level count. An extraction that reads one count per arm and outcome would not find these row-level counts.

What substituting the outcome-level count does to a comparison between arms depends on how the counts differ. If a row's count is the same fraction of the outcome-level count in both arms, a risk ratio computed from either count is unchanged but a risk difference is not; if the fractions differ between arms, both change. The post hoc analysis in amendment 4 measured these changes for two-arm outcomes. For outcomes posted as counts of participants, the changes were usually small, and below the threshold that amendment 4 fixed before the analysis was run: the median shift in the risk difference was 0.37 percentage points, and 2.0% of intervals changed their relation to zero. They were not negligible everywhere: about one comparison in eleven moved by more than 5 points, and about one risk ratio in five by 10% or more. For mean differences the error runs one way. In this cohort the outcome-level count was never smaller than the row's own, so a meta-analysis that used it would give the comparison too much weight, by a median of 12%. Most affected values were in outcomes with three or more groups, which the analysis did not cover.

Existing systems solve much of the restructuring. According to its archived source code at a revision of 20 October 2025 [31], AACT keys outcome groups within each outcome, stores counts as integers, and loads each outcome's type of units analyzed and its outcome-level counts with their units. For baseline measures it takes the number analyzed from the row, then the measure, then the overall count; for outcome measures nothing in its loader reads the counts that rows report. The live AACT database was not queried for this study, and the deployed release may differ. CTG-DB keeps one denominator per arm, the number of participants who started it, for its cross-trial adverse event analyses [21]. EXACT extracts posted results into analysis-ready spreadsheets for meta-analysis, although its users still converted some formats by hand, such as percentages to numbers of patients with events; the paper does not describe how it handles row-level or unit-specific counts [18]. Shi and Du labeled analysis groups as intervention or comparator by semantic similarity to the design arms, and matched adverse event arms to efficacy arms by exact string and then by similarity [19]. Such links identify arms, not analysis populations: in this cohort an outcome group and the identically titled baseline group had different participant counts for 53.6% of outcome groups where both counts were usable, so a link across modules should not be used to carry a denominator. ctrdata keeps each record as a document in the registry's own structure, which preserves the information; apart from one derived field that reports the number of subjects in the statistical test of the first primary endpoint, it leaves the choice of denominator to each analysis [20]. I did not run ctrdata. What this study adds is a measured account, value by value, of which denominator applies, with the rule named and the source location kept, and a separate check that the transformation kept every in-scope source value of this snapshot with identical text.

For evidence-table builders, the results support the following practices. Keep row-level numbers analyzed and use them before the outcome-level count. Keep the analysis unit and its count beside the participant count. Do not join groups across modules or outcomes by code or title. Record whether an outcome has a population description, but do not treat its absence as an error, because the field is required only when the number analyzed differs from the number assigned [24]. Treat "NA" and formatted numbers as text until a person decides what they mean.

Strengths and limitations

The cohort is a complete frame of three condition queries over a defined posting window, analyzed with rules fixed before a single held-out run. Each value keeps its source pointer, the transformation was reconciled value by value against a second implementation in R that reads the raw pages, and every module was rebuilt from the warehouse. The comparator kept values as text and copied every ancestor scalar down to each row. Its losses follow from the fields and denominator levels it omits, rather than from changes to the source text.

The fidelity reference is a second implementation in another language, but it was written with the same AI coding assistants, in the same project and from the same rules, and no other person reviewed either implementation. A shared misreading of those rules would therefore remain undetected. The pre-freeze AI review reported three shared departures from the plan or the rules: both implementations omitted a tier C case, gave tiers to measurements of outcomes that were not posted, and applied an upper bound on counts that the rules did not state. All three were resolved before the freeze (amendment 3, items 1 to 3). The rules decide which count applies from the record's structure; they do not establish that the registry entry is clinically correct, and no external gold standard exists for this question. The checks flag possible inconsistencies only where units and populations permit; they are not error rates.

The cohort covers three condition groups and one snapshot of a mutable registry. Records change, and the documented API serves only the most recent version of each record [25]. Earlier versions can be retrieved from the registry website, for example with the cthist R package [32], but this study did not use them; its raw pages and their hashes are the snapshot. The condition rule is a vocabulary rule, not clinical adjudication. The cohort is what the registry's condition search returned for three queries; I did not assess the search's recall, and confirmed studies include trials in which one of these conditions is listed beside another focus.

The flat comparator is one declared design. Its misattachments follow from keeping one outcome-level count per arm and outcome, so they measure how often a row reports a different number analyzed; a flat export that also carried each row's number analyzed and each unit count would not show them, and other designs may lose other information.

The measurements whose row reported a different number analyzed were concentrated in a minority of studies (post hoc). Results are finite-corpus descriptions of the held-out portion and should not be read as estimates for all registered trials. Free-text time frames were kept as text rather than converted into durations. The ledger includes a review instrument for future adjudication, but no item was adjudicated in this study. The consequence analysis was added after the results were known and after AI agents had seen rough magnitudes. It covers only outcomes with two groups, and its threshold for a material consequence is a judgment fixed in amendment 4, not an external standard. Its R recomputation came from the same AI model and the same written specification, so it checks the arithmetic, not the design.

Conclusions

In this cohort of interventional diabetes, heart failure and obesity trials with results first posted on ClinicalTrials.gov from 2020 to 2026, almost every posted value carried a participant count that could be attached by written rules. About one outcome group in seven carried that count at row level or beside a unit count, and a flat export that keeps one count per arm and outcome attached the outcome-level count in place of a different row-level number analyzed to nearly a quarter of measurements. In a post hoc analysis of two-arm comparisons, this substitution moved most risk differences little, although about one in eleven (9.3%) moved by more than 5 percentage points, and it raised the meta-analytic weight of mean differences by a median of 12%. Registry-based evidence pipelines should keep every denominator level with its provenance and choose among them by explicit rules.
