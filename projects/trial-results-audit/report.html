Attaching participant denominators to ClinicalTrials.gov results: a cross-sectional methods evaluation of a relational warehouse and a flat export in cardiometabolic trials

Animesh Bose, Norlowe Research

This report has not been peer reviewed.

Supplementary material: analysis plan and rules (Additional file 1), data dictionary (Additional file 2), source archive (Additional file 3), result tables workbook (Additional file 4), issue ledger, claims map.

Abstract

Background. Evidence tables built from ClinicalTrials.gov posted results need the number of participants behind each reported value. The registry records this at several levels (per outcome, per table row, and in units other than participants), which a flat export with one count per arm and outcome cannot hold. I measured how often written rules alone can attach a participant denominator to each posted value, and what a flat export loses or attaches instead.

Methods. I retrieved from ClinicalTrials.gov all interventional studies returned by its condition search for diabetes mellitus, heart failure or obesity with results first posted from January 2020 to September 2026. Studies were split by identifier cluster into development (508 studies) and held-out (1,961 studies) portions. Rules and code were frozen after development and run once on the held-out portion. Results were loaded into a typed PostgreSQL warehouse that keeps each value's source location. Each outcome group was classed as directly structured (A), needing an explicit rule (B), not safely resolvable (C) or not applicable (D). A separately written R program checked every warehouse value against the raw records. A declared one-row-per-value flat export served as comparator. Analyses were descriptive.

Results. Of 43,715 applicable held-out outcome groups, 37,592 (86.0%) had a directly structured denominator, 6,118 (14.0%) needed an explicit rule, mostly for row-level numbers analyzed, and 5 (0.01%) were not safely resolvable. For 22,505 of 97,756 measurements (23.0%), the flat export attached the outcome-level count where the row reported a different number analyzed. The warehouse reproduced all 2,502,817 in-scope source values exactly, and the R reference agreed with it on the tier and denominator of every measurement.

Conclusions. In this cohort almost every posted value could be given a participant count by written rules. About one outcome group in seven needed row-level or unit-specific handling, and a flat export with one count per arm and outcome attached an outcome-level count that differed from the row's own to nearly a quarter of measurements. Pipelines that build evidence tables from registry results should keep every denominator level with its provenance.

Keywords: ClinicalTrials.gov; trial results; denominators; analysis population; data provenance; relational database; evidence synthesis; research methodology

Background

ClinicalTrials.gov has accepted summary results of registered trials since 2008 [1,2], and the Final Rule extended results reporting to more trials from 2017 [3]. The results records are now reused at scale: systematic reviewers extract posted values [4], meta-researchers analyze reporting across thousands of trials [5], and tools re-publish the data in other forms. Posted results have also been used to emulate rapid safety meta-analyses [6] and to build a benchmark of 12,967 drug-versus-drug adverse event comparisons from 33,701 trial records [7]. A posted value means little without the number of participants it describes. Zarin and colleagues described the outcome measure as the "numerator" of a result and the analysis population as its "denominator", and found that the number of distinct analysis populations per study group averaged 2.5 across 700 early results records [1].

Studies comparing registry results with other sources keep returning to the denominator. Hartung and colleagues found primary outcome results inconsistent with the publication in 20% of sampled trials, including inconsistent analysis denominators and transposed arms [8]. Comparisons with trials published in high-impact journals also found results that could not be compared directly because the two sources reported them in different forms [9,10]. Schwartz and colleagues found the number analyzed for the primary outcome discordant with U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) reviews for 31% of primary outcomes, in a sample of approval trials of which 42% were for diabetes [11]. Riveros and colleagues found the number analyzed per group reported in 96% of sampled posted results but reasons for exclusion in only 8% [12], and Dechartres and colleagues could recompute effects from posted per-arm values for only 65% of trials that posted neither an estimate nor a p-value [13]. Registry staff have explained that results tables resist spreadsheet download because their rows and columns vary from record to record [14], and that internal inconsistencies between parts of a record are among the most frequent problems at results quality review [14,15].

Several tools already restructure posted results. AACT (Aggregate Analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov), built by the Clinical Trials Transformation Initiative [16], now loads the whole registry into PostgreSQL every day [17]. EXACT (EXtracting Accurate efficacy and safety information from ClinicalTrials.gov) extracts results into spreadsheets for meta-analysis [18], Shi and Du built an arm-level knowledge graph of efficacy and safety results [19], and the ctrdata package stores registry records, including results, as documents for analysis in R [20]. A study of the registry's protocol metadata found untyped coded fields, values that differed from the data dictionary and condition terms outside Medical Subject Headings (MeSH); it did not examine the summary results [21]. These systems show that restructuring is feasible. None of them reports how often each posted value can be paired with its own participant count without unsupported inference, or measures what a simpler representation attaches in its place.

The registry's own definitions make the question concrete. Each posted outcome has an "Overall Number of Participants Analyzed" per group; a table row can carry its own number analyzed "if different from the overall"; outcomes analyzed in eyes, clinics or other units report a unit count beside the participant count; and the analysis population description is conditionally required [22]. A representation that keeps one count per arm and outcome cannot hold these distinctions.

I built a typed, provenance-preserving warehouse for a frozen cohort of cardiometabolic trials and asked: among posted results, how often can a documented participant denominator be preserved and mechanically associated with each reported value without unsupported inference, and what does a declared one-row-per-value flat export lose or attach instead? This is a study of machine-readable reporting and transformation fidelity. It does not assess treatment effects, detect misconduct or adjudicate conditions clinically.

Methods

Aim, design and setting

This cross-sectional methods evaluation used one snapshot of the ClinicalTrials.gov registry (data timestamp 2026-10-06). Its aim was to measure how often written rules can attach a participant denominator to each posted results value without unsupported inference, and what a flat export attaches instead. Rules were developed on one portion of the cohort and run once on a held-out portion.

Data source and cohort

I used the ClinicalTrials.gov data application programming interface (API), version 2.0.5 [23,24]. The data timestamp was 2026-10-06T09:00:05 Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) both before and after retrieval, which began at 2026-10-07T09:33:06Z. Three condition queries were passed verbatim to the registry's condition search ("diabetes mellitus", "heart failure", "obesity") with the filter AREA[StudyType]INTERVENTIONAL AND AREA[ResultsFirstPostDate]RANGE[2020-01-01,2026-09-30]. The window refers to the date results were first posted after quality review, chosen before retrieval as 2020 through the last complete calendar month; it is not the study completion date. Every query was paged to the end and its identifier count checked against the API's reported total (1,511, 468 and 842 studies). The union of 2,615 studies was below the prespecified ceiling of 5,000 studies, so no sampling was needed. Full records were retrieved in batches of 100 identifiers, and every raw page was saved with its Secure Hash Algorithm 256-bit (SHA-256) hash. Responses that were not JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) were rejected.

The registry's condition search also matches titles, keywords and MeSH ancestor terms. I therefore confirmed each study's condition group by a deterministic, prespecified rule: the condition MeSH or ancestor terms contained the group's descriptor (Diabetes Mellitus, Heart Failure or Obesity), or a free-text condition entry matched the group's pattern. Studies whose condition entries and condition MeSH terms did not meet the rule (146 studies; returned through titles, keywords, other MeSH terms or condition text the rule does not accept) were set aside and used only in a sensitivity analysis. This is a vocabulary rule, not clinical adjudication. No study failed the frame checks (interventional, results present, results first posted inside the window), leaving 2,469 confirmed studies (1,440 diabetes, 435 heart failure, 762 obesity; 166 in more than one group).

Development and held-out portions

Before any results field was parsed, studies were linked into clusters when they shared an alias National Clinical Trial (NCT) number, the ClinicalTrials.gov identifier, an organization study identifier within the same organization, a secondary identifier, an identical official title, or near-identical brief titles (token Jaccard similarity of at least 0.9; 32 pairs). A first pass let bare values such as "2017", entered as secondary identifiers, join unrelated studies; identifiers with fewer than five alphanumeric characters were then ignored, and the change was recorded before any results were read. The 2,525 clusters were assigned by a salted SHA-256 hash of their smallest NCT identifier, about 20% to development: 508 confirmed studies for development and 1,961 held-out.

The loader, rules, checks and analysis code were developed and reviewed on the development portion only. Rule wording and an additional check of category sums were recorded after profiling the development portion (analysis plan, amendment 2). Two further checks, of adverse event group totals and of non-zero values with zero participants analyzed, were also defined on the development portion before the freeze; a note added to the plan after the held-out run records them. The development code was then checked against the written rules in a structured review, each defect found was re-checked before it was accepted, and the resulting corrections were recorded before the freeze (amendment 3). The files that define the computation were then hashed (combined SHA-256 5bd267b67dc1f28e…, 2026-10-07T10:53:24Z), and the primary held-out analysis was executed once. The program refuses a second primary run and allows only a logged reproducibility rerun, which writes to a separate directory and is compared with the original tables. A timestamped analysis plan written before retrieval, with dated amendments, is given in Additional file 1. It is an internal record, not a public preregistration.

Warehouse

Results were loaded into native PostgreSQL 17 tables for studies and module-scoped groups; participant flow periods, milestones and reasons not completed; baseline denominators, measures, rows, categories and values; outcome measures, groups, outcome-level and row-level denominators, rows, categories and values; statistical analyses with their comparison groups; and adverse event groups, terms and counts.

Group identifiers are keyed within their module, and outcome group identifiers within their outcome measure, so the same code in two outcomes is never assumed to be the same population. Every value is stored as the exact source text, with a parsed number only when the text is a plain decimal written in American Standard Code for Information Interchange (ASCII) digits; every row stores its JSON pointer into the source record.

Composite keys and foreign keys enforce the nesting. A load replaces the studies in scope inside one transaction, so loading a frozen snapshot twice gives identical tables, and a failed load leaves the previous state intact.

The results point-of-contact block (names, email addresses, telephone numbers) and the certain-agreement block (restrictions on investigators publishing results) were not loaded. A staging table keeps the rest of each record as retrieved, including the protocol section; the analysis does not use it. The source-to-warehouse data dictionary is given in Additional file 2.

Attaching a denominator

For each posted outcome measurement I applied written rules, in a fixed order (Additional file 1). The analysis unit is the outcome's selected unit, or participants when none is given. I treated an association as made without unsupported inference when it used only counts the record states for that group, with the precedence the registry's data element definitions give: a row reports its own number analyzed only "if different from the overall" count [22], so when no row reports one the outcome-level count applies to every row, and a row's own count takes precedence where it is given.

Reading a count from free text, borrowing one from another outcome or module, or converting a non-integer entry such as "NA" was treated as unsupported (tier C). When only some rows of an outcome report their own count, the convention would give the other rows the outcome-level count, but the record cannot show whether those rows were left blank on purpose, so this case was also treated as unsupported.

Tier A: one usable participant count for the group at the outcome level, no row-level counts, analysis in participants. Tier B: a deterministic rule is needed, either because the outcome's rows carry their own number analyzed, which then takes precedence (B1), or because the outcome is analyzed in another unit and the participant count is held separately from the unit count (B2). Tier C: no participant count can be attached without unsupported inference. This covers no count for the group (C1), a count that is not an integer, for example "NA" (C2), more than one count (C3), row-level counts on only some rows (C4), row-level counts only in other units (C5) and no usable count in the selected unit (C6). Tier D: the outcome is not posted or the group has no values.

An outcome group takes its least favorable measurement tier. The primary estimand is the distribution of held-out outcome groups across tiers A, B and C; tier D is reported separately.

Whether the outcome has an analysis population description is reported alongside, as present or absent; absence is not an error, because the field is required only when the number analyzed differs from the number assigned [22]. Population labels such as "modified intention to treat" are themselves used inconsistently [25], so the rules never read the denominator from the description text.

Comparator

The comparator is a declared flat export in the shape produced by json_normalize along each module's deepest list: one comma-separated values (CSV) row per value, with every scalar of its ancestors (module, outcome or measure, group, row, category) copied down, values kept as text, and the outcome-level participant count attached to each row. A declared export was needed because the registry's own CSV download carries no posted results data, only a flag for whether results exist and the date they were first posted [26].

A source value counts as recoverable from the flat files only when exactly one row matches its full context and a cell holds the identical text. Statistical analysis rows carry no context below their outcome, so they were matched by their order within the outcome, and only when the outcome's 13 descriptive fields (type, title, description, population description, time frame, units and similar) taken together are unique in the study (analysis plan, amendment 2, item 4, named type, title and time frame; the code requires the combination of all 13 fields to be unique).

Fidelity reference

A separate program in R (version 4.5.1, jsonlite 2.0.0) read the same raw pages, enumerated every scalar under each results section with its JSON pointer, counted every array, and implemented the written tier rules on its own. Agreement between the warehouse and this reference measures transformation fidelity. Because both follow the same written rules, a shared misreading of those rules would not be detected. I also rebuilt each results module from the warehouse and compared it with the source record as canonical JSON.

Checks for possible source inconsistencies

Twelve checks (K1 to K12) were run only where units and populations permit them. They looked for participant counts above their participant denominator (K1), unit counts above their unit denominator (K2), interval limits out of order (K3), a point value outside its interval (K4) and percentages of participants outside 0 to 100 (K5). For adverse events they looked for participants affected above participants at risk (K6) or above the number of events (K7), and group totals above participants at risk (K8). They also covered baseline counts above their denominator (K9), two-sided confidence intervals of statistical analyses whose limits are reversed or exclude the estimate (K10), non-zero values with zero participants analyzed (K11), and category counts that do not add up to the participants analyzed for their row (K12; categories are defined as mutually exclusive and exhaustive [22]).

Values that were present but not plain numbers were counted as not evaluable, as was a point value whose interval limits were out of order, since the limit-order check already reports that case. Reversed confidence limits of a statistical analysis, which the limit-order check does not cover, counted as a failure of the analysis check. A failure is a possible source inconsistency, not a confirmed error. Sums across rows, comparisons between outcome, baseline and participant-flow counts, and whether a mean, median or survival estimate should carry a binary event denominator were deliberately not checked. Every flag enters an issue ledger with its JSON pointer, the source values and the rule that fired.

Analysis

Counts are reported with their unit: study, study module, outcome, outcome group, measurement, source scalar, object or applicable check. The cohort is the complete set of confirmed studies among those the three queries returned, not a sample of all trials, and the held-out portion is a fixed hash partition of it. Percentages therefore describe this finite set of studies and carry no sampling intervals; a sensitivity analysis gives the shares for both portions combined. An exploratory bootstrap resampling whole trials (1,911 held-out studies with at least one applicable outcome group; 2,000 replicates, seed 20261007) shows how much the outcome-group tier shares depend on trial composition; its percentile range is not a confidence interval.

Four sensitivity analyses were fixed in advance: including ambiguous condition matches (SA1), primary outcomes only (SA2), counting a group as resolvable only if its outcome also has a population description (SA3), and both portions combined (SA4). I also described how often an outcome group's title exactly matches a baseline group title in the same study, and whether matching titles carry the same participant count. Among studies that use the group code OG000 in two or more outcomes, I counted those that give it different titles in different outcomes, comparing titles after case folding and whitespace collapsing. Results labeled post hoc were added after the held-out run (analysis plan, post hoc additions); they describe the held-out results further and change no prespecified result.

Manual verification

I reran the software tests and fixture demonstration from a clean extraction of the source archive. A stratified random sample of 30 held-out outcome groups was compared with the public ClinicalTrials.gov record pages: 10 in tier A, 10 using row-level counts, 5 using the unit rule and all 5 in tier C (seed 20261007). I also opened each of the 16 flagged possible source inconsistencies and the 5 unresolved outcome groups on its record page, recomputed headline counts and percentages from the exported tables, and checked each reference against its digital object identifier (DOI), PubMed record or web page.

Software and reproducibility

The pipeline (Python 3.13, PostgreSQL 17.11, R 4.5.1) runs as stages: acquire, freeze, build, analyze, validate, a post hoc stage, render and package. Software tests use seven synthetic studies with unused identifiers and are never mixed with registry data. Repeat loading, an independent rebuild into a second database and a rerun of the held-out analysis were each compared with the original outputs (Additional file 3).

Results

The held-out portion contained 1,961 confirmed studies (Table 1) with 19,957 outcome measures: 18,110 of 19,957 (90.7%) posted with values, 695 posted without values (all groups had zero participants analyzed or no rows) and 1,152 not yet posted. These outcomes had 43,715 applicable outcome groups and 97,756 measurements.

Most outcome groups have a directly structured denominator, and one in seven needs a rule

Of 43,715 applicable outcome groups, 37,592 (86.0%) were tier A, 6,118 (14.0%) tier B and 5 (0.01%) tier C; 1,885 groups were not applicable (Figure 1, Table 2). Most of tier B came from row-level numbers analyzed: all 5,578 groups in outcomes that reported them were B1, and in 4,795 of them at least one row's count differed from the outcome-level count (post hoc). In tables with more than one row, 5,641 of 13,399 groups (42.1%) needed a rule, against 1.6% of groups in single-row outcomes. Of 582 groups in outcomes analyzed in other units (for example clinics, eyes or samples), 540 used the unit rule (B2), 37 the row-level rule (B1) and 5 were tier C. These were all the tier C groups: in 3 studies whose units were clinics or community organizations, the participant count was "NA" while the count of clinics or organizations was given.

At the measurement level, 62,373 of 97,756 measurements (63.8%) used the outcome-level count, 34,579 (35.4%) a row-level count, 799 (0.8%) the participant count of an outcome analyzed in another unit, and 5 measurements could not be given one. No held-out measurement fell under the missing-count, duplicate-entry, partial-row, other-unit-row or missing-unit rules (C1 and C3 to C6).

Of posted outcomes, 12,978 of 18,805 (69.0%) had an analysis population description: 62.8% of primary and 71.9% of secondary outcomes.

What a flat export attaches

For 62,363 of 97,756 measurements (63.8%) the flat export attached the count that applies (Figure 2). For 22,505 (23.0%) it attached the outcome-level count while the row reported a different number analyzed, and for 12,074 (12.4%) the row-level count happened to equal it. For 799 (0.8%) it attached a participant count to values reported per clinic, eye or other unit; that count is correct as a participant count, but it is not the denominator of those values. For the 5 measurements with no safe count, its count cell held the source text "NA", and 10 measurements could not be located uniquely in the flat file. In total, 23,309 measurements (23.8%) carried an entry that was not the most specific participant count, was not in the unit of the value, or was "NA". The 22,505 measurements whose row reported a different number analyzed came from 385 of the 1,911 held-out studies with posted values (20.1%); the ten studies contributing most accounted for 8,450 of them (37.5%), and among measurements of primary outcomes the share was 2,260 of 20,271 (11.1%); these three descriptions are post hoc. The flat export also had no row at all for 1,847 of 19,957 outcomes (9.3%): the 1,152 outcomes not yet posted and the 695 posted without values. No outcome with posted values lacked a row.

Information retained by each representation

The warehouse returned every in-scope source value exactly. Of the 1,342,202 source values in the modules the flat export attempts, the flat export returned 1,162,192 (86.6%) exactly and under an unambiguous context (Figure 3, Table 3). Its losses were concentrated: it had no place for row-level denominators (78,727 values), baseline measure and row denominators (31,040), reasons participants did not complete (26,253) or outcome denominators in other units, and it lost the groups and fields of outcomes without value rows. Fields of the measurements themselves (group identifier, value, spread, limits and comments) were nearly always recoverable (>99.9%), as were statistical analyses (100.0%), although 70,892 of the 101,757 analysis values (69.7%), in 1,805 outcomes with two or more analyses, could be matched only by their order (post hoc). Adverse events (1,160,134 values) are outside this flat export and were kept in full by the warehouse.

Warehouse fidelity

The warehouse held every one of the 2,502,817 in-scope source scalars with identical text at the same JSON pointer, with no extra values; 11,982 source scalars in the point-of-contact and certain-agreement blocks were excluded by design. Object counts agreed for all 33 array types (924,616 objects). The R reference and the Structured Query Language (SQL) views agreed on tier, rule, participant denominator and unit denominator for all 97,756 measurements and on the tier and rules of all 45,600 outcome groups, tier D included. All 9,805 results modules rebuilt from the warehouse were identical to the source, and the evidence view returned exactly one row per measurement. No study had to be set aside at load (Table 4). My checks described in Methods found no discrepancy between the warehouse outputs and the public record pages, the exported tables or the cited sources.

Same labels across modules do not mean the same population

Of 43,715 outcome groups with values, 34,788 (79.6%) had exactly one baseline group with an identical title. Where both counts were usable, the outcome's participant count differed from the matching baseline group's count for 18,645 of 34,784 (53.6%) outcome groups. Within studies, the code OG000 named differently titled groups in different outcomes in 377 of 1,770 studies (21.3%) that used it in two or more outcomes. Joining modules or outcomes by group code or title would therefore often attach a count from a different group or analysis population. The same fact can also sit in more than one module, as Earley and colleagues showed for deaths [27]. The warehouse lists same-titled baseline groups for context but does not link them.

Possible source inconsistencies

The twelve checks were applicable 536,743 times; 314 applicable checks were not evaluable, almost all because a value was not a plain number and the rest because an interval's limits were out of order (reported by the limit-order check instead). In total, 16 checks failed (Table 5): 13 values in 3 studies, labeled as percentages of participants, were negative and may be percentage changes reported under that unit; one confidence interval had its lower limit above its upper limit; one median lay outside its reported interquartile range; and one statistical analysis reported its confidence limits in reverse order. Category counts added up to the participants analyzed in every one of the 2,873 evaluable combinations of row and group. The issue ledger holds these 16 items, 5 unresolved outcome groups and 38 outcome values whose text was not a plain number (for example "2,137.12"), each with its JSON pointer and source values. No transformation defects were found.

The rule picks the most extreme such outcome, so the example is not typical; its study also contributed the most measurements whose row-level count differed from the outcome-level count (3,009 of 22,505, post hoc). Sensitivity analyses and runtime

The tier B share was 13.8% when ambiguous condition matches were included, 11.2% when only primary outcomes were analyzed and 13.7% when both portions were combined, against 14.0% in the primary analysis; tier C stayed below 0.1% in each. Requiring a population description as well left 72.4% of groups resolvable (Table 7). This composite is stricter than the registry's own requirement, because a description is required only when the number analyzed differs from the number assigned [22]; it shows how much the result depends on that definition, not how many groups lack a usable count. On the build machine (Apple M5 Pro, 48 GB memory), loading all 2,615 studies took 37 seconds in one transaction; the warehouse tables, indexes and staging copy of each record occupied 557 MB against 164 MB of raw JSON, and the R reference's enumeration took 8.9 minutes.

Discussion

In 1,961 held-out diabetes, heart failure and obesity trials with results first posted from 2020 to 2026, a participant denominator could be attached by written rules to 99.99% of applicable outcome groups. In this cohort, the structured results were therefore usable for this purpose for almost every outcome group. The difficulty lies in where the count sits: 14.0% of groups, and 36.2% of individual measurements, carried their count at row level or beside a unit count. A flat export with one count per arm and outcome attached the outcome-level count in place of a different row-level number analyzed to 23.0% of measurements, and a participant count without the unit count to another 0.8%. Once an export omits row-level counts, its users cannot recover those counts from the exported values alone.

These results extend the denominator framing of Zarin and colleagues [1] from a sample of early records to every posted value in a recent cohort. Schwartz and colleagues found the number analyzed discordant with FDA reviews for about a third of primary outcomes [11]; part of such discordance may arise when one source reports a row-level count and the other an overall count, although these data cannot show how much. Dechartres and colleagues could not recompute effects from posted per-arm values for 35% of trials that posted neither an estimate nor a p-value, mainly because binary outcomes were posted only as percentages, continuous outcomes lacked a mean and standard deviation, or the data were censored or repeated over time [13]. Missing participant counts were not among the main reasons they named, and in this cohort these counts were almost always present. For 23.0% of all measurements, however, and 11.1% of measurements of primary outcomes (post hoc), the row reported its own number analyzed and it differed from the outcome-level count. An extraction that reads one count per arm and outcome would not find these row-level counts.

Existing systems solve much of the restructuring. According to its archived source code, AACT keys outcome groups within each outcome, types counts as integers and derives a baseline number analyzed from row, measure or overall counts. At the archived revision I inspected, however, it did not map outcome row-level denominators or the outcome's selected analysis unit [28]. I did not run AACT, and the deployed release may differ. EXACT extracts posted results into analysis-ready spreadsheets for meta-analysis, although its users still converted some formats by hand, such as percentages to numbers of patients with events; the paper does not describe how it handles row-level or unit-specific counts [18]. Shi and Du labeled analysis groups as intervention or comparator by semantic similarity to the design arms, and matched adverse event arms to efficacy arms by exact string and then by similarity [19]. Such links identify arms, not analysis populations: in this cohort an outcome group and the identically titled baseline group had different participant counts for 53.6% of outcome groups where both counts were usable, so a link across modules should not be used to carry a denominator. ctrdata keeps each record as a document in the registry's own structure, which preserves the information; apart from one derived field that reports the number of subjects in the statistical test of the first primary endpoint, it leaves the choice of denominator to each analysis [20]. I did not run ctrdata. What this study adds is a measured account, value by value, of which denominator applies, with the rule named and the source location kept, and a separate check that the transformation kept every in-scope source value of this snapshot with identical text.

For evidence-table builders, the results support the following practices. Keep row-level numbers analyzed and use them before the outcome-level count. Keep the analysis unit and its count beside the participant count. Do not join groups across modules or outcomes by code or title. Record whether an outcome has a population description, but do not treat its absence as an error, because the field is required only when the number analyzed differs from the number assigned [22]. Treat "NA" and formatted numbers as text until a person decides what they mean.

Strengths and limitations

The cohort is a complete frame of three condition queries over a defined posting window, analyzed with rules fixed before a single held-out run. Each value keeps its source pointer, the transformation was reconciled value by value against a separately written R program that reads the raw pages, and every module was rebuilt from the warehouse. The comparator kept values as text and copied every ancestor scalar down to each row. Its losses follow from the fields and denominator levels it omits, rather than from changes to the source text.

The fidelity reference was written separately, in another language, but follows the same rules. A shared misreading of those rules would therefore remain undetected. The rules decide which count applies from the record's structure; they do not establish that the registry entry is clinically correct, and no external gold standard exists for this question. The checks flag possible inconsistencies only where units and populations permit; they are not error rates.

The cohort covers three condition groups and one snapshot of a mutable registry: records change, and the API offers no programmatic access to earlier versions, so the raw pages and their hashes are the snapshot. The condition rule is a vocabulary rule, not clinical adjudication. The cohort is what the registry's condition search returned for three queries; I did not assess the search's recall, and confirmed studies include trials in which one of these conditions is listed beside another focus.

The flat comparator is one declared design. Its misattachments follow from keeping one outcome-level count per arm and outcome, so they measure how often a row reports a different number analyzed; a flat export that also carried each row's number analyzed and each unit count would not show them, and other designs may lose other information. The warehouse and the reference were developed within the same project, so their independence rests on separate code in separate languages.

Measurement-level shares are concentrated in a minority of studies (post hoc). Results are finite-corpus descriptions of the held-out portion and should not be read as estimates for all registered trials. Free-text time frames were kept as text rather than converted into durations. The ledger includes a review instrument for future adjudication, but no item was adjudicated in this study.

Conclusions

In this cohort of interventional diabetes, heart failure and obesity trials with results first posted on ClinicalTrials.gov from 2020 to 2026, almost every posted value carried a participant count that could be attached by written rules. About one outcome group in seven carried that count at row level or beside a unit count, and a flat export that keeps one count per arm and outcome attached the outcome-level count in place of a different row-level number analyzed to nearly a quarter of measurements. Registry-based evidence pipelines should keep every denominator level with its provenance and choose among them by explicit rules.
