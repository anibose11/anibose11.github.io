When hospital quality comparisons are not comparable

Animesh Bose, Norlowe Research

In Arizona, the hospital ranked first for heart-failure readmission could occupy any rank from 1 to 52 within the published intervals. I built a check that first establishes whether CMS records can be compared, then shows how much of their apparent ordering those intervals support.

Research question. When hospitals in one state are ranked on heart-failure readmission or mortality from the CMS Provider Data Catalog, how many requested records can enter a valid same-measure comparison, and how many of the resulting orderings do the published intervals separate?

  • 99.6%of 141,219 same-state orderings on heart-failure readmission were not separated by the published intervals
  • 97.0%for heart-failure mortality (138,228 orderings), against 62.0% for heart-failure excess days
  • 0 of 3,253hospitals with a heart-failure readmission rate in the current CMS files have a usable interval
  • 62.1%of 4,792 requested heart-failure readmission records passed every gate in the primary release

The data: three releases of the same measures

All data are public files from the CMS Provider Data Catalog: the hospital-level Unplanned Hospital Visits and Complications and Deaths files, Hospital General Information, the Footnote Crosswalk, Measure Dates, the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program file and the national and state reference files. I loaded each release with its own hospital list and dictionaries and pinned every file by SHA-256.

Primary measures are the heart-failure 30-day readmission and mortality rates. Heart-failure excess days in acute care is a supporting measure with a different unit (days per 100 discharges), and the acute myocardial infarction measures are a separate secondary analysis. No score is averaged across measures.

How the check works

Every hospital-measure-release record goes through eight gates in a fixed order: release status, identity, measure definition, population, observation window, numeric score, usable interval and footnotes. The tool records the first gate each record fails and keeps the source string next to every parsed value, so a suppressed score, too few cases and a hospital outside the reporting program stay distinct.

For records that pass, every pair of hospitals in the same state and measure is checked for separation: one hospital's upper limit must sit below the other's lower limit. Each hospital also gets an interval-compatible rank range, the ranks it could hold if every true value lay inside its published interval. The range uses the counting rule of Klein, Wright and Wieczorek (2020). Because CMS publishes marginal 95% intervals and no covariance, the range carries no probability statement and is not a simultaneous confidence set.

Why the release and comparison rules matter

The newest files could not be used for uncertainty

In the current files, released 2026-09-30, CMS shows "Not Available" for the intervals and national categories of 18 outcome measures, following a notice that issues had affected the published intervals and national comparison values. The previous release still carries the intervals the notice describes as affected. I therefore used the April 2026 refresh, the latest release with intervals not covered by a notice, and ran the two newer releases through the same gates to show what the check does with them: every record fails at the release gate, even though most hospitals still have a rate.

VA hospitals were moved to their own comparison after the plan was locked

The first version of the population gate excluded no hospital type. While checking a VA medical center that appeared in an example, I found CMS methodology text stating that Medicare enrollment criteria do not apply to VA hospitalizations and that VA data are a separate source. I recorded a dated amendment and applied the gate as written, so VA hospitals are compared only with other VA hospitals. The headline shares barely moved: readmission stayed at 99.6% and mortality went from 96.3% to 97.0%. The share of hospitals separated from no peer on mortality moved more, from 46.5% to 54.2%. Both runs are kept.

Results

In the primary release, 2,975 of 4,792 requested heart-failure readmission records passed every gate. Too few cases removed 1,118, and 59 records with valid-looking numbers failed because CMS marked them as covering only part of the performance period.

Among compatible hospitals in the same state, the intervals separated about one heart-failure readmission ordering in 257, and 87.3% of hospitals were separated from none of their peers. For mortality the intervals separated about one ordering in 34, and 54.2% of hospitals were separated from none. Excess days separated hospitals far more often, which matches earlier work comparing that measure with readmission. Weighting each state equally instead of each pair gave the same picture.

In all 48 states with at least 10 compatible hospitals, the hospital ranked first on heart-failure readmission could rank in the bottom half of its state. In Arizona, the widest gap between two hospitals' mortality intervals is 1.9 percentage points; that pair is one of 10 separated mortality pairs out of 1,199.

Between releases, the readmission measure kept its Measure ID but moved from a 36-month to a 24-month window and, under the FY 2026 final rule, added Medicare Advantage beneficiaries. The mortality and excess-days measures kept their identifiers too, but from the July 2026 refresh every study measure includes patients admitted with COVID-19 and uses updated risk adjustment. A comparison across those releases would mix definitions under one identifier.

Limitations

  • The primary release is not the newest; it is the newest whose intervals are not covered by a CMS notice, and its window ends in June 2024.
  • Separation is judged from marginal 95% intervals published to one decimal, without covariance. Overlap does not show that two hospitals perform the same, and non-overlap is not a formal test.
  • A statistical white paper commissioned for CMS cautions that indirectly standardized rates should not generally be compared directly between hospitals. I describe what the published intervals show for orderings people commonly make.
  • The VA exclusion rests on documentation I found after locking the plan; both runs are reported.
  • CMS national category counts exceed the counts in the hospital file by 137 to 229 hospitals per measure, and the public files do not identify the extra hospitals, so the reconciliation is incomplete.
  • The CMS notice does not describe the issues it reports, so earlier releases may also have been affected.
  • The results describe heart-failure and acute myocardial infarction outcome measures in the public files and say nothing about other aspects of hospital quality.

Reproduce the analysis

The source archive contains the code, SQL, configuration, the locked plan, tests and a synthetic demonstration. With Python 3.12 or later:

python3 -m venv .venv
.venv/bin/pip install -r requirements.lock -e .
.venv/bin/python -m pytest -q
.venv/bin/hospcomp demo
.venv/bin/hospcomp all

The demo runs on a small synthetic fixture and only shows that the software works. hospcomp all downloads the CMS files, stops if any file differs from the checksums pinned for the study, and rebuilds every table. The R cross-check needs R and is skipped without it; everything else needs only Python.
