What survives when drug labels are turned into FHIR medication records?

Animesh Bose, Norlowe Research

Research teams and drug-dictionary maintainers copy product identity from DailyMed labels and RxNorm into analysis tables and HL7 FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) records. I designed a study that measures, field by field, which identity and strength details survive that conversion for 1,475 current prescription labels of selected cardiometabolic drugs, and what common shortcuts lose. An update posted on October 25, 2026, adds a national count of how labels state strength and a comparison of the standard ways to carry it in FHIR R4 (below).

Update: which substance does the strength describe?

A label for atorvastatin calcium tablets states 10 mg of atorvastatin, not of the salt it lists, and FHIR Release 4 Medication has no element that records which substance a strength refers to. Analyses added on October 24, 2026, after the results below were known, measure how often labels state strength this way and how much each standard way of building FHIR R4 records keeps. They are post hoc and follow amendment A9. They were run, and their numbers recomputed with separate code, by AI agents (Claude Opus 5.5 via Claude Code), and they have not been peer reviewed.

  • In DailyMed's full release of October 23, 2026, 22,639 of 98,544 active-ingredient records (23.0%) in the 52,740 current human prescription labels state strength for a substance other than the one listed: 20,017 list a salt but state the moiety's strength, and 2,622 state strength for a different reference substance. They occur in 14,094 of the 52,439 labels that list an active ingredient (26.9%). Among products that list a salt but state the moiety's strength, amlodipine, amoxicillin and atorvastatin are the most common.
  • In the held-out records, 576 of 2,015 active ingredients (28.6%) listed a salt but stated strength for its moiety, most of them atorvastatin. FHIR R4 core read none of these strengths against the right substance.
  • The RxNorm code already in the records recovered the basis for 82.1% of them. With RxNorm's NDC history, which gave a single concept for 1,631 of 1,662 products instead of 1,285, it recovered 97.2%, at the cost of 101 ingredients that FHIR core had read correctly.
  • HL7-published extensions carried, by design, every attribute measured here, as the companion record does, with 0 validator errors and 16,914 context warnings. HL7's own SPL-to-FHIR transform completed for 1,029 of 1,036 label documents; it writes no strength-basis element, and it kept the basis for 98.3% of these ingredients only when its ingredient role codes are read as the guide intends.
  • Labels of one RxNorm clinical drug often code the ingredient differently: 23 of 102 clinical drugs with products from two or more labels. Each atorvastatin tablet strength appears under three codings.
  • Rescored by value instead of text, the flat export kept 47.3% of identity and strength fields, against 32.2% under the original scoring. Apart from the fields it never carries (product NDC, ingredient codes and basis), it loses the ingredients after the first and changes most package NDCs by padding them.

The full report has a section on these analyses. Their code, amendments and records are not yet in the downloadable source archive, which is the version archived on October 18, 2026.

The data

The corpus is every current DailyMed human prescription label whose products contain metformin, a sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor (empagliflozin or dapagliflozin), a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist (semaglutide or liraglutide), one of seven insulins, atorvastatin or lisinopril, including combination products. It holds 1,475 labels with 2,386 products, plus the preceding version of 218 labels. RxNorm came from the public RxNorm API (October 5, 2026 release). The pipeline cached every response with its URL, retrieval time and SHA-256 hash. Human regular, NPH (isophane) and 70/30 insulins sold without a prescription are filed as over-the-counter (OTC) labels and are outside this scope. Seven candidate labels in the 2006 to 2008 format had no structured product section and were excluded.

How it was measured

Each label was parsed into a lossless PostgreSQL layer that keeps NDCs with their hyphens, decimal strengths as written, and the class code that says whether a strength refers to the ingredient or its active moiety. Each label version was converted into a FHIR R4 Bundle of Medication resources, plus a companion record (a sidecar validated by a JSON Schema) for the details R4 has no element for. The official HL7 validator checked every Bundle. A reverse transform read the files back, and a second XML reader, a separate module that shares no code with the main parser, gave the comparison values. Labels were split by a fixed hash rule into development (30%) and held-out (70%) sets before tuning, and the matching rules were frozen before held-out labels were evaluated. Later fixes to the comparison code, the FHIR display text, the version comparison and one parser rule meant rerunning the affected stages. The analysis plan records each change; no matching rule changed.

Representing the label without changing its meaning

FHIR elements left empty where the source value does not fit

R4 Medication has a manufacturer element, but the labeler named in a Structured Product Labeling (SPL) document is often a repackager or distributor, and R4 states that the element is not meant for distributors. R4 also has a status element, but SPL marketing status describes Food and Drug Administration (FDA) listing and does not say whether a dictionary record is in use. Both elements were left empty in FHIR, and the source values were kept in the sidecar. The basis of strength also stayed out of FHIR core, because R4 Medication has no element for it. Read from FHIR core alone, 670 of 2,392 held-out active ingredients took the wrong basis-of-strength substance; the sidecar records the basis explicitly.

Comparison against a separate read of the source XML

The round trip was checked by software against a second XML reader written with a different parser and no shared code. On development labels this found faults in the reverse transform and comparison code. A repeated-moiety error found later affected both readers and required a documented correction. Both readers were written with AI coding assistance in the same project, so this is a software cross-check, not external validation. On October 24, 2026, AI agents (Claude Opus 5.5 via Claude Code) also compared the 30 records of the review sample with the DailyMed label pages and with freshly retrieved label XML; all 30 matched.

What the comparison found

  • The package-NDC crosswalk gave a unique RxNorm concept unique identifier (RxCUI) for 77.3% of held-out products; for the rest, RxNorm lists none of the product's package NDCs as active, mostly for repackaged products. DailyMed's own set-level mapping agreed with it in 1,269 of 1,270 checkable cases.
  • Matching normalized names plus strength, form, fill volume and brand agreed with the crosswalk for 99.2% of its answers. Approximate name matching disagreed with the crosswalk for 87 of its 565 answers (15.4%).
  • DailyMed's packaging service lists every strength in a label against every product of that label: 856 of 1,662 held-out products got the wrong ingredient count.
  • Insulin strengths are coded in three Unified Code for Units of Measure (UCUM) units across labels ([iU], U and [arb'U]). The validator accepted both [iU] and U, even though UCUM defines U as the unit of catalytic activity. The sampled terminology check included no [arb'U] codes.

Two worked cases show the detail: a combination tablet preserved in full and an insulin pen that neither name-based approach matched to the crosswalk concept.

Limitations

The study covers six ingredient groups at one retrieval date, with one preceding version per label for 218 sampled labels, and RxNorm only as its current release plus the dated NDC history it publishes. The crosswalk is a terminology reference with its own errors. The companion record was designed to hold what the round trip measures, so its complete preservation is partly by construction; the informative numbers are the losses without it. The October 24 update was defined after these results were known, and its checks were made by software and by AI agents; no pharmacist or terminology expert reviewed them. The national census counts how labels state strength, not how often products are used. None of this speaks to the clinical correctness of any label.

Reproduce the analysis

unzip medication-identity-source.zip && cd medication-identity
python3 -m venv .venv && .venv/bin/pip install --require-hashes -r requirements.lock && .venv/bin/pip install --no-deps -e .
.venv/bin/medid demo          # offline, about a minute, fixture labels only
.venv/bin/medid scope && .venv/bin/medid acquire && .venv/bin/medid pipeline   # full study

Needs Python 3.11 or later, PostgreSQL 16 or 17 server binaries on PATH (the pipeline runs its own cluster), and Java 17 or later with the HL7 validator jar (see the README's validator section). The full run downloads about 530 MB from DailyMed and RxNav.
