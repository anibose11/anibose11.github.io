A real quote can accompany a wrong trial result

Animesh Bose, Norlowe Research

When a language model answers a question about a clinical trial and backs it with a quote that really appears in the article, how often is the answer still wrong? The study measured this for small models that run on a laptop and, in a dated update below, for three frontier models, on every eligible test question of an expert-annotated benchmark.

Update: three frontier models, run on 24 October 2026

The same 1,209 test questions, prompt and scoring were run through three frontier models, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Claude Fable 5.1 and GPT-6.1 Sol, accessed through their vendors' APIs on 24 October 2026. They were far more accurate: with retrieved passages, 6.8% to 8.0% of their answers with a genuine quote had the wrong result, against 45.6% for Qwen3-1.7B. But 99.6% to 100% of their answers carried a genuine quote, and none of their wrong answers failed the quote check, so the check still could not tell right answers from wrong ones. For Claude Fable 5.1 and GPT-6.1 Sol, a correction based on two AI judges from another vendor put more than half of the remaining errors down to mistakes in the benchmark labels, which brings their rates to 3.5% and 3.0%; those judges are AI models, not expert raters. A second model that reread the quoted passage removed most errors of the small models, but the share of questions answered fell from 81.3% to 44.8%.

This comparison was added after the original results were known, in an amendment time-stamped before any frontier output existed. It has not been peer reviewed. The code and model responses are planned for the next version of the code archive.

Qwen3-1.7B backed 928 of its 1,196 answers with a quote that really appears in the passages it was given. Of those, 423 reported the wrong result (45.6%, 95% CI 41.8% to 49.3%).

Nearly half of the primary model's quote-backed answers were wrong

Each question names a treatment, a comparison treatment, and an outcome, and asks whether the trial found the outcome significantly higher, significantly lower, or not significantly different. Physicians answered all of them when the benchmark was built. With retrieved passages, Qwen3-1.7B parsed cleanly on 1,205 of 1,209 questions and its label matched the physicians on 651 of 1,196 answered questions (54.4%). Its quote existed in the passages for 77.6% of answers, and 45.6% of those quote-backed answers were wrong.

The mistakes had a clear shape. When the physicians had recorded no significant difference, 295 of 385 quote-backed answers were wrong, and every one of those errors reported a significant effect. Another 122 (28.8%) of the 423 quote-backed errors reversed the direction, reporting higher for lower or the reverse. In 123 (29.1%) of the errors the quote overlapped the sentences the physicians had marked as evidence, so missing the evidence explains only part of the mistakes; 47 of these reversed the direction while quoting that evidence.

Handing the model the physicians' own evidence sentences did not remove the problem: 29.2% of its quote-backed answers were still wrong. A TF-IDF classifier that simply attaches its top-ranked passage passed the quote check on all 1,209 questions. So did a majority-class guess with the same passage attached, even though it was wrong for 698 of them.

A larger model from a later Qwen3 release had a lower rate but did not remove the problem. Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 got 79.3% of the questions it answered right and still got 213 of 1,039 quote-backed answers wrong (20.5%). Because it differs from Qwen3-1.7B in release as well as size, this does not show that size alone made the difference. With retrieved passages, the smaller Qwen3-0.6B's labels were not clearly less accurate than Qwen3-1.7B's, but it often failed to copy a quote or to point to the right passage, so only 260 of 1,209 questions (21.5%) ended with an answer that passed every check.

Copying, source location, and answer correctness were checked separately

The model returned a label, a quote, and the ID of the passage the quote came from. The harness scored whether the output parsed, whether the model abstained, whether the quote existed in the supplied passages (at least four words, ignoring differences in spacing), whether the passage ID pointed to the passage that held it, whether the quote overlapped the evidence the annotators marked, and whether the label matched the benchmark. End-to-end success required an answer, an existing quote, a valid passage ID, and the correct label. Every question stayed in the denominator, including unreadable outputs. Evidence overlap was reported separately. A quote that exists is a check on copying. It does not establish that the sentence supports the label.

Two data findings changed how evidence was located

The corpus documentation describes evidence offsets as inclusive. On 3,000 training rows, slicing with an exclusive end reproduced the annotated text for about 9 in 10 rows and an inclusive end for none, so offsets were treated as half-open. The repository's folder of abstract files was the other surprise: 2,334 of its 3,285 files contain full articles. The abstract-only condition therefore used the title and abstract section of the same text the offsets refer to, which keeps every quote and every annotated span on one coordinate system.

The prompt wording, greedy decoding, and the device were chosen on 100 validation articles by rules written into the analysis plan, and the 1,024-token retrieval budget was set from validation recall, all before any language model saw a test question. The written analysis plan, its timestamp, and three dated amendments are in the archive.

Throwing out answers without a valid quote left the error rate where it was

A natural safeguard is to keep only answers whose quote checks out and points to the passage it came from. For Qwen3-1.7B with retrieved passages, that rule set aside 325 answers, 178 of them correct, and moved the error among the answers it kept from 45.6% to 45.7%. Setting aside the same number of answers at random, 2,000 times, gave a mean error of 45.6%. The rule cost about a quarter of the answers and did no better than discarding the same number at random. The same held for Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and for the other contexts, with one exception: when Qwen3-0.6B was handed the physicians' evidence sentences, the rule lowered its error from 34.8% to 29.8%, more than random discarding achieved.

Other signals carried some information. A simple scoring rule fitted on the validation answers, using six features of each answer such as how many outcome words the quote contains and whether its significance wording agrees with the label, ranked the test answers better than chance (area under the risk-coverage curve 0.337 against 0.450 for random order; lower is better). That rule is exploratory, and its scores are not probabilities.

What this does not show

  • Evidence Inference 2.0 is public and may be in the models' training data. A clean train and test split does not rule that out.
  • These are models of 0.6 to 4 billion parameters. Three frontier models were added later; see the frontier-model update above.
  • The rate depends on the prompt. On validation data, a prompt with a worked example gave a lower rate of wrong quote-backed answers, 35.3% (83 of 235) against 41.7% (120 of 288), but fewer valid quotes, and the rule fixed in advance chose the other prompt.
  • The test articles were published between 1993 and 2018. This older expert benchmark does not represent current literature.
  • Annotators marked one or two evidence spans per question. A quote outside them can still be valid evidence, so overlap with annotated spans is a partial check.
  • Retrieval was lexical (BM25) with a fixed budget. Better retrieval could change the retrieved-context numbers.
  • Error categories come from fixed rules rather than expert review, and a passing quote check does not test whether the quote supports the label.

Reproduce the analysis

The source archive holds the code, tests, frozen configuration, every recorded model response, item-level scores, and the analysis plan. Model weights and article texts are not included; the acquire stage downloads them at pinned versions.

python3 -m venv .venv
.venv/bin/python -m pip install -r requirements.lock.txt
.venv/bin/python -m pip install -e .
.venv/bin/python -m pytest -q
.venv/bin/ema demo --cached
.venv/bin/ema acquire && .venv/bin/ema build && .venv/bin/ema devselect
.venv/bin/ema baselines && .venv/bin/ema infer && .venv/bin/ema analyze
.venv/bin/ema validate && .venv/bin/ema render

The primary model took about 2.7 hours for 3,592 calls in float32 on an Apple M5 Pro GPU. The code also runs on CPU.
